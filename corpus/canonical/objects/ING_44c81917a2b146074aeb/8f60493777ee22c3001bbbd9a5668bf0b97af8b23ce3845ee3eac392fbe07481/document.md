## ABSTRACT

As cities continue to expand, the demand for effective underground infrastructure to address urban transportation challenges has increased. Tunnels, particularly those constructed using the New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM), play a crucial role in managing these challenges. This study investigates the deformation behaviors of NATM tunnels under three different cover depths (17 m, 26 m, and 56 m) using the Finite Element Model (FEM) under static loading conditions. The main objective is to analyze the impact of varying cover loads on the stability and structural integrity of egg-shaped tunnel geometries, which are commonly preferred by designers. The analysis investigates deformations during excavation, focusing on both tunnel convergence and surface settlement, in a four-layered ground profile consisting of weathered soil, weathered rock, soft rock, and hard rock, respectively. By comparing these deformations under different cover loads, this  study  highlights  critical  factors  influencing  tunnel  design  and provides valuable insights for ensuring the safety and efficiency of shallow tunnel construction, particularly in multi-layered rock formations. Key findings indicate a maximum vertical displacement of -11.50 mm at the top of the final lining and a maximum compressive thrust of 155 t/m in the deepest case, demonstrating a non-linear structural response to increasing cover depth. The results, supported by graphical representations, demonstrate the importance of precise cover load calculations in tunnel stability and contribute to the development of engineering guidelines for similar projects.

Keywords: Cover depth, Egg-shaped tunnel, Finite element analysis, Multi-layered rock formations, New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM), Tunnel deformation.

## 1. Introduction

The continuous expansion of cities has led to land shortages in large  metropolitan  areas,  bringing  forth  numerous  transportation challenges. Underground structures have proven to be an effective design  approach  for  addressing  traffic  problems  in  major  cities. These underground systems play a critical role in transportation networks, allowing for the efficient handling of many transportationrelated  issues  [1].  Tunnel  excavations  are  carried  out  in  various ground conditions, making tunnel projects closely linked with geotechnical and rock mechanics. Today, tunnel technology has become one of the most preferred types of transportation infrastructure, facilitating faster logistics and transportation services [2]. Tunnels, particularly in urban development, are commonly used for transportation and public utilities [3].

The New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM) is a widely applied technique in tunnel construction, where understanding the deformations that occur under various conditions is of paramount importance. Külekçi (2022) [4] emphasized that in the NATM, the excavation method, proper selection and timing of support elements, and accurate geological formation determination are crucial factors that significantly reduce construction costs and minimize potential risks. Tunnels are often subjected to different types of loading, such as static, dynamic, impact, or blast loads, which can result in significant deformations. These deformations may manifest on the tunnel surface, side walls, or crown. Numerous case studies have shown that the maximum deformation in circular tunnels typically occurs in the crown region [1]. The stability of the tunnel face in circular tunnels largely depends on the cover depth and diameter [5, 6]. Tunnel deformations  are  influenced  by  several  factors,  including  cover depth, mechanical strength of the rock mass, and the intensity of the imposed loads [7]. Studies on shallow tunnels under static and dynamic  loads  have  shown  that  crack  lengths  increase  significantly during static loading, and the strength properties of the rock have a considerable impact on the magnitude of deformation [8, 9].

In  their  study,  Akutagawa  et  al.  (2006)  used  a  strain-softening model to predict the deformation behavior around a shallow NATM tunnel. The results indicated that prediction accuracy improved in later stages, allowing for the determination of deformation mechanisms  with  reasonable  tunnel  parameters  [10].    Golshani  et  al. (2016) analyzed the effect of different cover loads on tunnel deformations in a 3D numerical model of the Hakim Tunnel in Tehran, Iran,  using  ABAQUS  software.  This  study  highlighted  the  importance of accurate tunnel behavior modeling and ensuring structural stability [11]. A numerical study on the Lowari Tunnel project in Pakistan demonstrated how different excavation sequences affect ground behavior, emphasizing the effectiveness of NATM in optimizing excavation strategies to minimize deformation [12]. Similarly, the application of NATM in the Bolu Tunnel under weak rock conditions demonstrated the method's adaptability and capability in maintaining tunnel stability [13]. Under the FEM framework, various studies have demonstrated the method's effectiveness in analyzing tunnel and cave stability under complex geological conditions. Alemdag et al. (2019) employed FEM to investigate the stability of the Akçakale Cave and the overlying Arsa neighborhood, revealing ceiling and sidewall displacements of up to 48 mm and identifying a high risk of local or sudden collapse, especially within the first 75 m of  the  cave  entrance  [14].  Similarly,  Franco et  al.  (2022)  utilized PLAXIS 3D software based on FEM to evaluate stability and deformation behavior at a tunnel portal, providing recommendations for optimal excavation and support strategies [15]. In another application, Wijaya et al. (2021) analyzed the deformation behavior of twin tunnels in the Cisumdawu Tunnel using 3D FEM and umbrella injection  protection,  showing  notable  settlement  and  lateral  deformations that underscored the necessity of comprehensive 3D analyses in twin tunnel designs [16]. Vijay Kiran Kota et al. (2021) used discrete element modeling to evaluate tunnel stability in jointed rock, considering different tunnel shapes and analyzing deformation patterns [17]. In their study, Adlım et al. (2022) examined the impact of tunnel geometry on deformations in shallow and layered rock formations.  The  findings  revealed  that  egg-shaped  tunnels  exhibited less settlement and convergence compared to other geometries, making them advantageous in certain geological conditions [2].

Surface subsidence is a common issue in tunnel construction beneath urban areas. To address this, Li &amp; Wang (2014) conducted numerical simulations to analyze the impact of large-section, closely spaced tunnel construction on surface deformations in a metro station in Dalian. The study demonstrated the significant effect of adjacent  tunnel  construction  on  surface  deformations  [18].  In  another study, Berdoudi et al. (2022) investigated deformations around underground  mining  structures  and  proposed  that  increasing  tunnel cover could significantly reduce surface subsidence, a finding critical for the design of stable underground mining operations [19].

Shallow  tunnels  constructed  in  soft  ground  are  particularly  affected by both static and seismic loads [20]. The large deformations occurring in shallow underground structures during tunnel construction  have  always  posed  a  threat  to  engineering  safety,  especially when the tunnel passes through areas with high stress concentrations, such as fault zones or weak rock formations [9].

Studies on the deformations of egg-shaped tunnels under different cover  loads  are  important  for  ensuring  tunnel  design  and  safety. These studies are essential for gaining a better understanding of tunnel stability and deformations. Boldini et al. (2005) analyzed the effects of changing in-situ geological and geotechnical conditions on tunnel linings in NATM tunnels, examining soil-lining interactions using realistic material models [21]. Akutagawa et al. (2008) proposed a stress-softening analysis method to understand deformation behavior in shallow tunnels, investigating the effects of key parameters in such tunnels [22]. Chen &amp; Zhu (2015) investigated the effect of train loads on deformations in large-diameter shield tunnels and simulated their effects on tunnel deformations [23]. Erbeyoğlu et al. (2017) applied egg-shaped tunnel design to certain sections of the Bahçe-Nurdağ (Fevzipaşa) variant tunnel passage, analyzing ground behavior and stress distribution with this design. This approach offers a significant solution for maintaining tunnel stability, particularly  in  complex  geological  conditions  [24].  Busby  &amp;  Marshall (2000) used innovative design and construction methods, including egg-shaped tunnel design and bridge lifting techniques, in the Øresund  Tunnel  project  [25].  Aygar  &amp;  Gökçeoğlu  (2020)  employed egg-shaped tunnel design in the Çukurçayır-2 Tunnel in Trabzon to overcome stability issues in weak soils [26]. Ha et al. (2021) developed an extended longitudinal deformation profile model that considers rock mass conditions during tunnel excavation, addressing inaccuracies  in  traditional  methods  through  3D  numerical  analyses [27].

The stability of tunnels is a complex issue due to the geometry, applied loads, and interactions between the rock and lining materials. Tunnel stability during and after excavation is a critical concern for designers, as any collapse can destroy large sections of a completed tunnel, leading to costly repairs and time delays. Therefore, pre-calculations are of great importance. By better understanding the mechanical properties and stress states in shallow sections of the rock mass, it is possible to determine which factors are important for a specific construction site, thus improving the cost-effectiveness of pre-investigations and tunnel construction [28].

In the face of rapid population growth, the demand for energy, transportation, and drinking water has increased significantly, necessitating  the  active  use  of  underground  spaces.  Overcoming  these challenges  is  only  possible  by  utilizing  tunnels  and  underground voids. Due to their geometric complexity, the loads acting on them, and the interactions between the rock and cover materials, tunnels are challenging to analyze. The stability of any underground structure is the primary concern during and after excavation, as any collapse could lead to costly repairs and delays. For this reason, preliminary design is of great importance. Tunnel projects often face risks due to the difficult environmental conditions and unexpected factors stemming from regulations. Therefore, the geometrically complex tunnel structures in modern engineering and tunneling studies can be modeled statically using the FEM, incorporating heterogeneous material properties.

In this study, parametric design and analysis were conducted using the TUNAPLUS FEM program. TUNAPLUS is an analysis software developed for NATM tunnel design and is used to calculate nonlinear three-dimensional systems [29]. The aim of this study is to investigate the deformations encountered during the construction of naturally  supported  tunnels  and  to  understand  how  these  deformations change throughout the defined excavation stages. Specifically, the impact of excavation stages on deformations will be analyzed in detail using the FEM. These analyses will provide valuable insights for design and engineering guidelines to ensure the safe and efficient construction of NATM tunnels. The study aims to evaluate the effect of three different cover loads (17 m, 26 m, and 56 m) on the deformations of an egg-shaped tunnel geometry in a specific rock class by loading these cover loads and designing the tunnel based on parameters used for the same rock soil, then measuring the effects of these different cover loads on deformations.

While there are studies in the literature on tunnels constructed using NATM and their associated deformations, what sets this study apart is the FEM of the tunnel under three different cover loads. Furthermore, although the egg-shaped tunnel geometry is commonly favored by designers, the comparative analysis of deformations (convergence within the tunnel and surface settlement) that may occur in shallow tunnel construction and in multilayered rock soils has not been sufficiently addressed in the literature [2]. While the existing literature  addresses  various  NATM  and  deformation  studies,  the core originality of this research lies in its integrated approach, which simultaneously evaluates the structural stability of the preferred eggshaped tunnel geometry against the combined effects of highly varying cover loads (17 m to 56 m) and a four-layered heterogeneous rock formation. This comprehensive analysis, which models 22 distinct excavation stages, provides a critical synthesis to address major engineering  uncertainties: the complexity  of  design  under  sudden cover  load  fluctuations,  the  ambiguities  inherent  in  construction stages, and the instability risks associated with multi-layered, heterogeneous rock masses. This work offers unique data on the non-linear  deformation  response,  which  is  insufficiently  documented  for this specific geometry under these complex site conditions. In this context, the effects of cover load on the structural integrity and stability of the tunnel have been evaluated, and deformations have been measured in detail. Additionally, the analysis results are supported by graphs and substantiated with data. Our study emphasizes the critical role of accurate cover load calculations and analyses in tunnel design, serving as a guide for methods to be employed in such engineering projects.

## 2. Materials and Methods

In this section, the investigation of tunnel lining deformations focuses on the interplay between various factors that significantly influence the structural behavior of tunnels. Understanding the deformations in tunnel linings, especially as related to cover depth, is crucial for ensuring the stability and safety of the overall structure. To address this, we have considered multiple key aspects, including the geological characteristics of the surrounding rock, the design of supporting elements, and the methods used during tunnel excavation and construction. Additionally, advanced numerical modeling techniques, such as finite element analysis, have been employed to provide deeper insights into the behavior of the tunnel under various loading conditions. The following sections elaborate on the methodologies adopted for these critical factors.

## 2.1. Properties of the rock formation

The cover depth (H) in tunnels is defined as the distance from the spring line to the surface. A FEM with consistent cross-sectional geometry and a layered rock profile is employed to analyze the effects of three different cover depths (Case I (17 m), Case II (26 m), and Case III (56 m)) on deformations and displacements, as illustrated in Figure 1(a). The geological formations consist of four layers, with the groundwater level located at a shallow depth of 10 m below the surface. This deliberate choice to locate the groundwater level near the surface was implemented to simulate highly critical ground conditions  that  frequently  challenge  tunnel  designers,  particularly  regarding the potential for reduced effective stress and the generation of hydrostatic pressure. In the FEM, the effect of water was fully coupled into the analysis, influencing the unit weight of the layers below the water table and contributing to the static water pressure and loosening load, which is critical for NATM lining design. This approach allows the parametric study to evaluate lining stability under one of the most adverse hydrogeological scenarios. From the top down, these layers include weathered soil, weathered rock, soft rock, and hard rock. The engineering properties of these layers, as depicted in Figure 1(b), include the following soil index properties: 𝛶 (Unit weight), RK0 (Coefficient of earth pressure at rest), E (Young's modulus), υ (Poisson's ratio), 𝜑 (Internal friction angle), C (Cohesion), and 𝜏 (Tensile strength). The magnitude of these parameters for each layer was normalized against the geotechnical guidelines and rock strength properties presented by Wyllie and Mah [30, 31]. This normalization ensured that the selected values are consistent with the characteristics  of  the  general  soil  and  rock  types  modeled  and aligned with empirical ranges commonly used in FEM of NATM tunnels [17]. These geotechnical parameters were determined based on a comprehensive rock mass classification (e.g., RMR/GSI) derived  from  typical  field  investigation  results  for  this  layered  formation and were subsequently calibrated with empirical literature values. This methodology ensures that the model parameters are representative of the expected rock mass behavior during excavation. The thicknesses of  these  layers  are  represented  as  H1  (weathered soil), H2 (weathered rock), H3 (soft rock), and H4 (hard rock), with H4 consistently set at 40 meters in all cases. The thicknesses of H1, H2, and H3 for each case are provided in Table 1.

## 2.2. Design of tunnel support elements

Temporary support elements, such as shotcrete and rock bolts, provide immediate stabilization by securing the surrounding ground and preventing collapses. Permanent support elements, including reinforcing bar and lining systems, ensure longterm integrity and resistance to various loads, including earth pressure and groundwater. The geometric and mechanical properties of these elements must be carefully evaluated to ensure they meet the specific geological conditions and loading scenarios of the tunnel site.

## 2.4. Selection of tunnel geometry

The selection of tunnel geometry is crucial in ensuring the structural integrity, safety, and efficiency of tunnel construction. Different  geometries  can  significantly  affect  the  distribution  of  stresses within the tunnel, influencing factors such as ground stability, ventilation, and construction costs.

In this study, the egg-shaped geometry was chosen due to several advantages  it  offers.  First,  the  egg  shape  efficiently  distributes stresses along the tunnel walls, reducing the risk of failures or collapses. Second, this geometry allows for improved hydraulic performance, making it suitable for areas prone to water inflow. Additionally,  the  egg  shape  provides  greater  headroom  and  width  at  the crown, facilitating the movement of machinery and personnel within the tunnel. ## 2.5. Finite element analysis with TUNAPLUS

The TUNAPLUS Analysis Program was utilized to perform finite element analysis for tunnel design, developed by Comtec Research [29]. TUNAPLUS is a specialized software designed for geotechnical  engineering  applications,  particularly  in  tunnel  design  and analysis. It offers advanced features for modeling soil-structure interaction, allowing engineers to assess the impact of excavation and support systems on tunnel stability. The program enables the simulation of different loading conditions and material behaviors, providing valuable insights into the structural performance of tunnels. Regarding the boundary conditions for the soil-tunnel interaction, a 2D plane-strain model was used, and standard constraints for static finite element analysis were applied: the bottom boundary of the model was fixed in both horizontal ( Ux = 0 ) and vertical ( Uy = 0 ) directions, while the left and right vertical boundaries were constrained only in the horizontal direction ( Ux = 0 ), allowing for vertical movement to simulate continuity with the far-field ground. ## 3. Numerical Findings and Discussion

This section presents the results of the finite element analysis, focusing on how the internal forces and deformations change in response to increasing cover depth across the three defined cases: Case I (17 m), Case II (26 m), and Case III (56 m). As the depth increases, the tunnel lining experiences varying levels of stress, which directly affect the bending moments, thrust forces, and shear forces, as well as the deformation patterns at critical points. The analysis evaluates how the tunnel's  structural  behavior  evolves  under  the  increasing load imposed by the deeper rock mass in each case, providing valuable insights into the load-bearing and deformation capacities of the tunnel lining as the cover depth increases.

## 3.1. Structural analysis of final lining

As the cover depth in tunnels increases, the interaction between the surrounding rock and the tunnel lining becomes more complex, influenced by excavation mechanics, material strength, and load-deformation  relationships.  Greater  cover  depth  results  in  increased overburden, leading to higher loads on the tunnel lining. This shift causes changes in internal forces, including bending moment, thrust, and shear force, which are analyzed and illustrated in Figure 5-7, and the critical values of internal forces are given in Table 5.

With shallow cover depths, such as in Case I (17 m), the load on the tunnel lining is relatively low, which is reflected in the bending moment and thrust values. The bending moment, both positive and negative, remains moderate because the forces acting on the lining are not extreme. The structure undergoes bending, but the stress is manageable since the surrounding rock mass is not exerting excessive pressure. The compressive thrust (45 t/m) and tensile thrust (20 t/m) are also relatively low, indicating that the rock's ability to transfer the load onto the lining is limited. As the cover depth increases to 26 m in Case II, the load increases significantly. The bending moment rises to 4.17 tm/m, suggesting that the lining is experiencing greater flexural stress. The increased overburden causes more compression and tension in the tunnel structure, which is evident in the higher compressive (75 t/m) and tensile (47 t/m) thrust values. The greater thrust indicates that the tunnel is subject to higher axial forces, resulting from both the weight of the rock and the deformation of the surrounding material. This also leads to a slight increase in the shear force, as the interaction between the tunnel and the rock mass becomes more intense. At the greatest cover depth in Case III (56 m), the structural forces acting on the tunnel lining reach their peak. The compressive thrust increases drastically to 155 t/m, while the tensile thrust reaches 117 t/m. These high values suggest that the lining is under severe compressive and tensile stresses. The deep rock layers exert significant pressure, which transfers to the tunnel lining, causing it to experience large deformations. The bending moment becomes highly negative (-4.17 tm/m), indicating that the tunnel structure  is  undergoing  more  extreme  bending,  particularly  in  regions where the rock's  confinement  causes  the  lining  to  resist  the  load. Moreover, the increase in shear force to 8 t/m highlights the growing role of shear stresses at greater depths. The rock mass, under such high loads, exerts not only axial forces but also lateral forces that contribute  to  shear  deformations  in  the  tunnel  structure.  This  increase  in  shear  forces  reflects  the  higher  risk  of  structural  failure through shear-related mechanisms, especially in deep tunnels where the rock is more likely to deform unevenly around the tunnel lining.

As the cover depth increases from Case I to Case III, there is a significant rise in both the inner-outer concrete fiber and reinforcing bar stresses, which aligns with the earlier observations of moment, thrust, and shear forces (Figure 8, 9). In shallow tunnels (Case I), the stresses are comparatively low, with the maximum inner extreme fiber stress at 123 t/m² and the outer reinforcing bar stress at 1284 t/m². However, as the depth increases in Case II and Case III, the stresses rise  significantly,  with  maximum  inner  concrete  fiber  stress  and outer reinforcing bar stresses reaching 459 t/m² and 3772 t/m², respectively, in Case III. The calculated stress in the reinforcing steel (3772 t/m²) remains well below its yield stress (250 MPa = 25,484

t/m²), corresponding to a safety factor of approximately 6.76. In contrast, the inner concrete fiber stress (459 t/m²) exceeds the allowable design limit of 𝜏 = 300 t/m², yielding a safety factor of 0.65. Therefore, while the steel reinforcement demonstrates sufficient capacity, the concrete lining in Case III is overstressed and requires design modification to withstand the elevated compressive loads. These increases in stress reflect the higher mechanical demands placed on the tunnel lining due to greater loads from the surrounding rock mass. The minimum stress values also rise, particularly in the deeper tunnel, indicating that even the tensile forces on the structure grow significantly with depth.

In this study, critical nodes were identified to assess the deformation behaviour of the tunnel lining. At shallow depths, such as in Case I (17 m), the rock above the tunnel is less constrained, and the deformation is relatively small. For example, the vertical displacement on the surface (Node 1) is - 3.85 mm, which reflects the downward movement of the ground in response to the tunnel excavation. This displacement is modest because the rock layers are not heavily compressed at this depth, allowing for more uniform stress redistribution without causing excessive subsidence. However, as the cover depth increases to 26 m in Case II, the vertical displacement at the surface increases to -4.45 mm. This indicates that the weight of the overburden is more significant,  causing  more  pronounced  downward  movement.  The  surrounding rock mass is now subject to greater loads, and as it deforms, it transfers this load onto the tunnel lining, causing additional vertical displacement both at the surface and within the tunnel structure. By the time we reach Case III (56 m), the vertical displacement at the surface slightly decreases to -4.02 mm. This reduction might seem counterintuitive, but it can be explained by the increased confinement provided by the deeper rock layers. At such depths, the rock mass can behave more like a rigid body, limiting surface subsidence even though the internal deformations (within the tunnel) become more significant.

For the tunnel lining itself, the deformations are more pronounced. The top of the final lining (Node 2) shows significant vertical displacement as the cover depth increases, from -6.90 mm in Case I to -11.50 mm in Case II. This sharp increase in displacement reflects the growing compression at the top of the tunnel, where the load from the overburden is concentrated. The rock mass is pushing down on the tunnel, causing the lining to compress and deform more severely. By Case III, the displacement at the top of the lining slightly reduces to -10.01 mm, which can be attributed to the more confined behaviour of the deeper rock layers, which again leads to reduced displacement at extreme depths. An additional safety assessment focused on crown settlement was conducted using the criterion proposed by Ding et al. [32], which suggests a maximum allowable relative crown settlement of 0.10% of the tunnel diameter (D). Given the tunnel's diameter (D = 2 × R1 = 13m), the critical crown settlement limit is 13 mm. Since the maximum observed vertical settlement at the lining crown is 11.5 mm (Case II), all three modelled scenarios are deemed acceptable, indicating that the overall stability condition for this specific deformation mode is within safe limits for Case II.

At the bottom of the final lining (Node 3), vertical displacement also follows a similar pattern. In Case I, the displacement is 3.01 mm, indicating some upward movement as the lining reacts to the excavation. As the depth increases, the left side of the lining experiences more stress, leading to greater deformation, reaching 6.95 mm in Case III. The increased upward displacement here is likely due to the tunnel's interaction with the surrounding rock mass, which causes the lining to deform more in response to the uneven pressure distribution as the depth increases.

In contrast, the horizontal displacements show a different trend, especially on the right side of the lining. In Case I, the right side of the final lining (Node 4) experiences a small horizontal displacement of 0.56 mm, which increases to 0.72 mm in Case II. This indicates that as the tunnel becomes deeper, the surrounding rock mass pushes more laterally on the tunnel lining, causing horizontal movement. However, by the time the cover depth reaches 56 m in Case III, the horizontal displacement reduces significantly to 0.20 mm. This reduction can be explained by the increasing stiffness of the deeper rock layers, which restrict lateral movement and cause the surrounding  material  to  act  more  uniformly,  reducing  horizontal  displacements. A similar trend is observed at the left side of the tunnel lining (Node 5), where the horizontal displacement in Case I is -0.56 mm, increasing to -0.72 mm in Case II, and then dropping to -0.20 mm in Case III. The increased displacement in Case II suggests that the lining is experiencing more lateral pressure from the rock, causing it to deform horizontally. However, as the tunnel gets deeper in Case III, the surrounding rock mass becomes more rigid, reducing the amount of horizontal deformation that can occur.

Overall, the deformations in the tunnel structure, both vertical and horizontal,  are  driven  by  the  load-deformation  relationship  as  the cover  depth  increases.  At  shallow  depths,  the  tunnel  experiences more manageable and uniform deformations, with modest vertical and  horizontal  displacements.  As  the  depth  increases,  the  tunnel structure is subjected to higher stresses, particularly vertical compressive forces, leading to more severe deformations, especially at the top and sides of the lining. However, at greater depths, the increased confinement of the rock mass begins to reduce horizontal displacements, as the deeper layers of rock restrict movement and behave more uniformly.

## 4. Conclusion and Recommendations

This study systematically analyzed the deformation behavior of tunnel linings under varying cover depths (Case I: 17 m, Case II: 26 m, and Case III: 56 m) using finite element analysis. The findings revealed that the maximum vertical displacement at the top of the final lining increased significantly from -6.90 mm in Case I to -11.50 mm in Case II, and slightly decreased to -10.01 mm in Case III. This indicates that while the overburden pressure increases with greater cover depths, the structural response exhibits a non-linear relationship, potentially due to the effects of confinement in deeper cases. A safety check based on Ding et al. [32] (limit: 0.10% D = 13 mm) shows that the maximum crown settlement (11.5 mm in Case II) remains below the allowable limit, confirming safe performance in all cases.

The analysis of horizontal displacements at the right side of the final lining showed a peak value of 0.72 mm in Case II, contrasting with a decline to 0.20 mm in Case III. This behavior suggests that increased cover depth may lead to a redistribution of lateral forces, with the deeper rock mass in Case III providing enhanced confinement that significantly restricts lateral deformation.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed:

- Incorporate the observed displacement trends into the design of tunnel linings, particularly for scenarios with significant overburden. Consider  enhancing  reinforcement  techniques,  especially  in  areas prone to higher vertical displacements.
-  Implement  real-time  convergence  monitoring  at  the  tunnel crown (Node 2) and the spring lines to ensure the vertical settlement remains below the critical limit of 13 mm (0.10% D), thereby guaranteeing  serviceability  and  structural  stability  during  construction and operational phases.
- Conduct additional numerical simulations to assess the impact of varying material properties and loading conditions. This can provide insights into potential failures or excessive deformations that may occur under unforeseen circumstances.
- Prior to construction, perform comprehensive geotechnical assessments to understand the soil behavior and rock mass characteristics that may influence tunnel performance, ensuring that designs are tailored to site-specific conditions.
- Although this study focused on the effect of static cover load, the impacts of dynamic (seismic) loads and long-term creep behavior should be considered in comprehensive future research to further enhance the reliability of underground structures.
- Finally, an important limitation of this study is the assumption of a constant groundwater level (at 10 m below the surface) across all three varying cover depth scenarios. While this simplification was necessary to isolate the effects of the cover load (the primary variable), it is acknowledged that a dynamic groundwater regime-where the water table elevation typically varies with depth, topography, and seasonal changes-would  significantly influence the effective stresses and hydrostatic pressure on the tunnel lining. Therefore, the absolute  numerical  results  derived  from  this  comparative  model should be interpreted as representing the structural behavior under a single, conservative hydrogeological boundary condition.

These recommendations aim to enhance the understanding and management of tunnel behavior under different excavation conditions, ultimately improving safety and structural integrity in tunnel design.
