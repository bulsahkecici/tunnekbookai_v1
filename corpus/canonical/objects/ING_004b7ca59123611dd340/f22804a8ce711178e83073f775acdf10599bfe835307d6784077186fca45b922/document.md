## Model test on longitudinal differential settlement of operational shield tunnel under localized surface loading

Yucheng Zhao 1,2 , Zeyang Wang 3 &amp; Qingnan Lan 4 

Shield tunnels are constructed by assembling precast segments with bolts, resulting in relatively weak longitudinal load-bearing capacity. Consequently, under surface loading, operational shield tunnels are prone to longitudinal differential settlement, which can induce structural distress and adversely affect normal operation. This manuscript first examines the patterns of longitudinal differential settlement in shield tunnels under surface loading through engineering case studies, analyzing the deformation and failure characteristics of the tunnel segment structure. Subsequently, detailed similitude model tests were conducted, fully accounting for joint effects, to investigate the influence of factors such as tunnel burial depth, soil properties, and surface loading on the differential settlement of operational shield tunnels. Finally, based on the test results, the longitudinal deformation behavior and earth pressure distribution characteristics of shield tunnels under various influencing factors were analyzed. The findings indicate that: The greater the tunnel burial depth, the more pronounced the soil arching effect, when the burial depth exceeds 3D, the influence of local loading on longitudinal settlement is minimized. The magnitude of longitudinal differential settlement decreases as the load offset distance increases; when the offset distance is less than 2D, the impact of local loading on the shield tunnel becomes significant. Compared to longitudinally uniform soil layers, when the tunnel traverses a soft soil interlayer, its maximum settlement increases sharply; moreover, the lower the compression modulus of the soft soil layer, the greater the settlement. The research outcomes aim to provide valuable references for the design and operational management of shield tunnels.

Keywords Shield tunnel, Model test, Longitudinal uneven settlement, Local surface load, Longitudinal heterogeneous soil layer

With sustained economic development, urbanization levels and urban populations are steadily rising. Major cities are experiencing severe traffic congestion. Metro systems, leveraging their core advantages of ultra-high capacity,  speed,  punctuality,  and  resource  efficiency,  have  become  an  effective  solution  for  alleviating  traffic congestion in modern high-density cities (Shi et al. 1 ;  Wang  et  al. 2 ).  Currently,  the  shield  tunneling  method and  TBM  method,  known  for  their  fast  excavation  speed,  high  degree  of  mechanization,  and  construction safety, are widely used in the construction of metro tunnels and hydraulic tunnels (Tang et al. 3 ; Huang et al. 4 ; Zhou et al. 5 ; Han et al. 6 ; Lai et al. 7 ). However, the structural integrity of shield tunnels is inherently reduced, as the segments are connected by bolts in both the circumferential and longitudinal directions. Under external construction disturbances, such as surface surcharge loading and foundation pit excavation, operating shield tunnels are prone to longitudinal differential settlement. When the tunnel deformation reaches a certain degree, it can induce common defects such as segment cracking, segment misalignment, and joint leakage, significantly impacting the normal operation of the metro system (Hu et al. 8 ; Meng et al. 9 ; Salimi et al. 10 ; Armetti et al. 11 ).

In recent years, numerous researchers have conducted studies addressing the issue of longitudinal differential settlement in shield tunnels. The aforementioned research indicates that numerous factors contribute to differential settlement in shield tunnels, which can generally be attributed to internal structural factors and external environmental loads. Internally, the discrete segmental joint configuration of shield tunnels results in longitudinal stiffness discontinuity. When a tunnel traverses geologically heterogeneous strata-such as transitioning from stiff clay to soft silt or crossing bedrock protrusions-it becomes susceptible to differential settlement. Externally, changes in environmental loads are primarily triggered by construction activities in the vicinity of the shield tunnel. For example, adjacent excavation pits, surface surcharge or unloading, and groundwater level fluctuations can alter the stress field, displacement field, and seepage flow field around the tunnel, imposing uneven additional stresses on the tunnel structure.  Once  longitudinal  differential  settlement  occurs  in  a  shield  tunnel,  it  can  easily  induce  structural defects  such  as  segment  cracking  and  bolt  failure.  Furthermore,  significant  non-uniform  deformation  may compromise segment gaskets, allowing groundwater from the surrounding soil and rock mass to infiltrate the tunnel through seepage pathways, leading to water leakage issues (Zhang et al. 18,19 ). Currently, research on the longitudinal differential settlement of shield tunnels under the influence of multiple factors remains limited, and further investigation is warranted.

In the field of shield tunnel model testing, He et al. 20 and Wei et al. 21 conducted full-scale loading tests to investigate the failure mechanisms and bearing performance of shield tunnel segments under various loading conditions. Huang et al. 22 utilized  transparent  soil  models  to  propose  failure  modes  of  tunnel  excavation  faces  across varying soil conditions and burial depths. He et al. 23  executed model tests on homogeneous, heterogeneous, and internally heterogeneous segment structures, revealing the influence of internal configurations and longitudinal forces on the longitudinal mechanical behavior. Xu et al. 24 conducted shaking table tests on shield tunnels in soft soil, hard soil, and soft-over-hard strata, highlighting differences in seismic response across soil types. Wei et al. 25 implemented full-scale tests on three-ring staggered assembly segments, identifying internal force response characteristics  underground  load  disturbances.  Zhang  et  al. 26 adopted  reduced-scale  laboratory  model  tests to examine longitudinal deformations under varying additional stress conditions. These studies indicate that while prototype testing can yield relatively accurate results, it is typically limited to single-ring loading tests, focusing primarily on the ultimate bearing capacity and failure characteristics of shield tunnels. Currently, many researchers employ acrylic plates to simulate tunnel segments. Although this material offers advantages such as ease of processing and convenient observation of internal deformation, it struggles to accurately represent the mechanical properties of concrete structures. When conducting model tests, the influence of shield tunnel segment joints on longitudinal differential settlement is significant, and the simulation of these joints cannot be neglected. Furthermore, soil parameters are also crucial for assessing the longitudinal differential settlement of shield tunnels (Zhou et al. 27,28 ; Rostami et al. 29 ; Jain et al. 30 ; Frough et al. 31 ).

In  summary,  this  study  primarily  investigates  longitudinal  differential  settlement  of  operational  shield tunnels under partial surcharge loads. Section 2 investigates the patterns of longitudinal differential settlement in shield tunnels under surface loading through an engineering case study, and analyzes the deformation and failure  characteristics  of  the  tunnel  segment  structure.  Section  3  carries  out  detailed  similarity  model  tests considering joint effects, examining the influence of factors such as tunnel burial depth, soil properties, and surface loading on the differential settlement of operational shield tunnels. Section 4 analyzes the longitudinal deformation behavior and earth pressure distribution characteristics of shield tunnels under various influencing factors. Section 5 discusses the limitations of the study and suggests directions for future research. The findings provide critical insights for improving shield tunnel design and operational management.

## Causes of longitudinal uneven settlement in shield tunnels Case background

The section between Chuangxin Middle Road Station and Huaxia East Road Station on Shanghai Metro Line 2 is located in Pudong New Area. Construction was completed in October 2008, as shown in Fig. 1. This shielddriven tunnel is a single-track, single-bore tunnel with a depth of 16 m below ground. The shield tunnel has an outer diameter of 6.2 m, inner diameter of 5.5 m, and ring width of 1.2 m. It employs precast concrete segments assembled via longitudinal and circumferential bolts with straight joints. Each ring comprises one top-sealing block, two adjacent blocks, two standard blocks, and 1 invert block. Geotechnical investigations indicate that the shield tunneling primarily traversed clay layers (Layer ④ and ⑤ ), as depicted in Fig. 2(a). According to on-site engineering records, sudden localized surface loading occurred above the shield tunnel section in December 2008, with an average height of 4.0 m and a maximum height of 7.0 m. The surface surcharge area was primarily positioned above the right tunnel, with its toe aligned approximately with the edge of the left tunnel-suggesting that the overload did not directly impact the left tunnel, as shown in Fig. 2(b).

A sudden increase in surface load alters the original stress equilibrium of the soil, resulting in additional earth pressure on the shield tunnel, which leads to localized differential settlement deformation and may ultimately cause damage to the segment structure. As shown in Fig. 4(a), under surface loading, the shield tunnel tends to deform from a circular shape into an ovalized shape. The inner side of the tunnel crown is often subjected to high tensile stress. If longitudinal joints are present at the crown, bolts in this area may experience tensile fracture, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). If the crown consists of solid segments, this area is prone to concrete spalling and cracking, as depicted in Fig. 5(b). Conversely, the inner side of the tunnel springline typically experiences high compressive stress. The concrete at both ends of the longitudinal joints at the springline acts as a rotational pivot, where concentrated compressive stress leads to surface concrete crushing, as shown in Fig. 5(c). Since the shield tunnel is longitudinally connected by bolts and can be regarded as a slender structural component, a sudden increase in surface load makes it more susceptible to differential settlement, which further causes ring joint opening, as presented in Fig. 4(b). When the ring joint opening exceeds a certain threshold, the sealing gasket at the joint fails. In areas with abundant groundwater, water from the surrounding soil may infiltrate the tunnel through these gaps, with leakage at the haunche being the most severe (Zhang et al 33,34 .), as illustrated in Fig. 5(d).

## Model test scheme

## Similarity relations in model testing

The  above  evidence  indicates  that  localized  surface  loading  can  readily  induce  structural  defects  in  shield tunnels.  Accordingly,  this  study  conducts  a  model  test  to  investigate  the  differential  settlement  behavior  of shield tunnels under localized surface loading, considering the influence of longitudinal soil properties. Model testing is a scientific method that involves constructing physically or mathematically similar models of prototype systems to simulate real-world conditions under controlled environments. This approach aims to acquire data, validate theoretical models, or optimize design parameters. To ensure consistency in the governing physical laws between the model and the prototype, model testing must satisfy the three similarity theorems (Wang et al. 35 ; Guo et al. 36 ). In this study, geometric similarity ratio C L = 25 and a physical similarity ratio C F = 25 are adopted as the fundamental similarity ratios. Similarity ratios for other physical parameters are derived using dimensional analysis. The corresponding similarity constants used in the model tests are summarized in Table 1.

## Shield tunnel model

Simulation of shield tunnel segments

Shield tunnel segments are made of C50 concrete, with an elastic modulus of 34.50 GPa. The analysis of test material characteristics revealed that properties of high-strength gypsum are similar to those of concrete. model materials with different elastic moduli can be prepared by mixing other materials with high-strength gypsum. Diatomite, a hydraulic material with good water absorption and workability, acts as a retarder when added to gypsum. Moreover, it can address the excessive porosity issue faced during the hydration process of highstrength gypsum. Therefore, high-strength gypsum and diatomite are selected to simulate the concrete material of the segments. The ratio of the model materials used is water: high-strength gypsum: diatomite = 1:1.8:0.1. A uniaxial compression test was conducted to obtain the load-deformation curve of the model material, as shown in Fig. 6(a). The calculated average elastic modulus of the three groups of model materials is 1.29 GPa, with an error of less than 7% compared to that of C50 concrete. Steel reinforcement in the shield tunnel segments is primarily simulated using iron wire. A wire mesh with a diameter of 0.4 mm is arranged on both the inner and outer surfaces of the model (three longitudinal bars and 24 circumferential bars), as shown in Fig. 6(b).

## Simulation of bolt connections for shield tunnel segments

Segment bolts  are  critical  structural  connectors  in  shield  tunnel  construction,  used  to  fasten  precast  lining segments. They are primarily classified into circumferential and longitudinal types, with circumferential bolts typically being longer, as they interconnect segment rings. These bolts, through straight or curved/bent designs, assemble segmented blocks into an integrated tunnel structure. They play a critical role in maintaining structural stability and controlling ground settlement. Bolt failure may induce increased joint deformation, waterproofing failure,  segment  misalignment,  and  or  even  structural  instability.  Consequently,  their  influence  cannot  be neglected in shield tunnel model tests.

## Fabrication and assembly of shield tunnel segments

The prototype shield tunnel has an outer diameter of 6200 mm, segment thickness of 350 mm, and ring width of 1200 mm. Based on the similarity requirements, the corresponding dimensions of the model tunnel are an outer diameter of 250 mm, segment thickness of 15 mm, and ring width of 50 mm. The fabrication of the shield tunnel model follows a series of steps: segment molding, rebar installation, material mixing, segment casting, segment drying, cutting ring joints, drilling longitudinal holes and bolt installation, and segment assembly. The resulting shield tunnel model, as shown in Fig. 9, comprises 30 rings assembled with staggered joints.

## Loading and monitoring

To ensure uniform transmission of surface load, a 25 cm × 25 cm steel plate is placed on the soil surface to simulate a foundation, with standard weights placed on top of the steel plate. The surface load is applied in six levels (200 to 1200 kPa), using weights of 50 kg each, as shown in Fig. 12. To ensure measurement accuracy, the setup is left undisturbed for 1 h after each loading and unloading phase of the experiment, allowing the tunnel structure to stabilize before proceeding to the next set of experiments.

In the model test, the main parameters to be monitored are the settlement of the segments and the pressure of the soil above these segments. The monitoring scheme is as follows: uneven settlement monitoring points for the shield tunnel are arranged at the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th rings. Settlement data is transmitted to WY-3 type displacement sensors through pre-embedded measuring rods and sleeves in the soil, as depicted in Fig. 13(a). Precision soil pressure cells (with a range of 500 kPa) are placed at the crown positions of the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th rings. The soil pressure cells are taped to the corresponding positions on the segments. Additionally, at the 15th ring, soil pressure cells are embedded laterally every 15 cm, as shown in Fig. 13(b).

## Model test process

The entire test process is as follows: (a) Machine oil is applied inside the test box to minimize the influence of boundary friction. (b) The test box is filled with sand and clay up to a height of 0.5 m from the bottom, with each layer compacted to a thickness of 0.25 m. (c) The shield tunnel model is carefully installed through pre-reserved holes in the test box to prevent damage to the tunnel segments from excessive external forces. (d) Pressure cells are attached at the corresponding positions on the tunnel segments and soil pressure cells are embedded in the soil. (e) The longitudinal settlement monitoring system is installed, including displacement sensors, measuring rods, and sleeves, ensuring that the bottom end of the sleeve is vertically aligned with the crown of the shield tunnel. (f) The monitoring system is checked, initial readings of the displacement sensors and soil pressure cells are recorded, and the soil is continuously filled up to the specified height. (g) The loading plates are installed and the load is applied in six stages. After each loading stage, the setup is allowed to rest for 1 h to reach equilibrium, and then the test data promptly recorded. The entire test process is illustrated in Fig. 14.

## Analysis of test results Impact of surface load offset distance

The load-settlement curves at each measurement point of the shield tunnel under different surface load offset distances are shown in Fig. 15(a) to (e). The longitudinal settlement curve of the shield tunnel under a surface load of 1200 kPa is presented in Fig. 15(f). It should be noted that all data shown in the figures are modelgenerated  values.  From  the  figures,  it  can  be  observed  that  when  the  tunnel  burial  depth  (H = 2D)  and  the longitudinal homogeneous soil layer comprises 5% (water content) sand, the crown settlement values at various positions along the tunnel structure exhibit a linear variation with the increasing surface load. When the surface load reaches 1200 kPa, the maximum crown settlement values are 6.14 mm (S = 0D), 4.39 mm (S = 1D), and 1.85 mm (S = 2D), respectively. As the surface load offset distance decreases, the additional stress induced in the surrounding soil gradually increases, making the shield tunnel more susceptible to uneven settlement. At a surface load of 1200 kPa, compared to the offset distance (S = 0D), the settlement decreases by 28.3% for (S = 1D) and by 69.7% for (S = 2D), indicating that when the surface load offset distance is greater than (S = 2D), the influence of the surface load on the uneven settlement of the shield tunnel becomes minimal.

When the load offset distance is (S = 0D) and the longitudinal soil layers comprise 5% (water content) sand, 50% (water content) clay, and 5% (water content) sand, the crown settlement values at various positions of the tunnel structure exhibit a linear variation with increasing surface load. When the surface load reaches 1200 kPa, the maximum crown settlement values are 12.44 mm (H = 1D), 11.09 mm (H = 2D), and 3.31 mm (H = 3D), respectively. As the burial depth increases, the shield tunnel gradually transitions from a shallow-buried tunnel to a deep-buried tunnel, resulting in the soil arching effect. The stress acting on the tunnel structure transitions from loose pressure to deformation pressure. Therefore, with increasing burial depth, the settlement values of the shield tunnel gradually decrease. Under a surface load of 1200 kPa, compared to the burial  depth (H = 1D), the crown settlement values at (H = 2D) and (H = 3D) decrease by 10.8% and 70.6%, respectively. This indicates that when the burial depth exceeds (H = 3D), the influence of the surface load on the uneven settlement of the shield tunnel becomes minimal.

The distribution curves of longitudinal crown soil pressure under different burial depths are shown in Fig. 18 (a), and the transverse soil pressure distribution curves at the crown position of the shield tunnel are illustrated in Fig. 18 (b).

From the figures, it can be observed that under different burial depths, the soil pressure induced at the crown of the shield tunnel is symmetrically distributed. At the 14th ring, the maximum vertical soil pressure values at the crown are 43.4 kPa, 35.3 kPa, and 9.7 kPa for burial depths (H = 1D, H = 2D, and H = 3D), respectively. Compared to a burial depth of (H = 1D), the vertical soil pressure at the crown of the shield tunnel decreases by 18.7% at (H = 2D) and by 78.3% at (H = 3D). When the burial depth is less than (H = 1D), the surface load has a significant impact on the shield tunnel. Under different surface load offset distances, the transverse soil pressure distribution at the crown position of the shield tunnel becomes uneven. The closer the surface load is to the shield tunnel, the greater the soil pressure on the tunnel crown. Within the transverse monitoring range at the crown position of the shield tunnel, the vertical soil pressure decreases as the transverse distance increases. These observations highlight that the effect of surface loads on shield tunnel settlement diminishes significantly as the burial depth increases beyond (H = 3D), and the distribution of soil pressure becomes more uniform and less impactful on the tunnel structure.

## Impact of longitudinal soil layer properties

Load-settlement curves at each measurement point of the shield tunnel under different longitudinal soil layer properties are illustrated in Fig. 19(a) to (e). The longitudinal settlement curve of the shield tunnel under a surface load of 1200 kPa is depicted in Fig. 19(f). When the surface load offset distance is (S = 0D) and the burial depth is (H = 2D), the crown settlement values at various positions of the tunnel structure exhibit a linear variation with increasing surface load. When the surface load reaches 1200 kPa, the crown settlement values under uneven longitudinal soil layers are as follows: 10.01 mm (40% clay), 11.09 mm (50% clay), and 11.98 mm (60% clay). The crown settlement value of the tunnel structure increases with a decrease in the elastic modulus of the soil layers it traverses. Under a surface load of 1200 kPa, compared to the homogeneous soil layer with 5% sand content, the maximum crown settlement values of the shield tunnel structure in soil layers with 60% clay increased by approximately 83.3%, indicating that uneven longitudinal soil layer distribution is more likely to cause uneven settlement of the shield tunnel under surface loading.

The distribution curves of longitudinal crown soil pressure under different longitudinal soil layer properties are depicted in Fig. 20 (a), and the transverse soil pressure distribution curves at the crown position of the shield tunnel are illustrated in Fig. 20 (b). From the figures, it can be observed that under surface loading, when the shield tunnel longitudinally crosses soil layers with different properties, the distribution trend of crown soil pressure is basically consistent. At the 14th ring, the maximum vertical soil pressure values at the crown are 36.7 kPa, 34.5 kPa, and 35.4 kPa, respectively. The differences among these values are small, ranging from 34 kPa to 37 kPa. These observations indicate that although the settlement behavior of the shield tunnel is influenced by the longitudinal soil layer properties, the soil pressure distribution at the tunnel crown remains relatively stable and consistent across different soil conditions.

## Discussion

This study has provided valuable insights into the behavior of shield tunnel structures through experiments on both transverse and longitudinal structural characteristics, as well as soil-structure interaction models using sandy soils. However, there remain several areas that warrant further investigation.

- 1 The transverse and longitudinal stiffnesses of the shield tunnel structure are interdependent, as evidenced by the experimental results. In this study, the transverse and longitudinal stiffnesses were treated separately. Future research should consider the effect of the annular joint stiffness on the longitudinal stiffness, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the structural performance under operational conditions.
- 2 Soil consolidation is a complex and prolonged process. In this study, each load level was applied for only one hour, ignoring the time-dependent effects of soil settlement. Consequently, the data obtained is limited, as it does not fully capture the long-term effects of consolidation. Future studies should account for the time-dependent behavior of soil consolidation to enhance the accuracy and relevance of the results.
- 3 While the effects of loading on the tunnel's crown settlement were considered, the transverse deformation of the tunnel was not measured due to limitations in the available experimental equipment. The transverse deformation can significantly affect the overall tunnel stability, especially under varying loading conditions. Future experiments should incorporate measurements of transverse deformations to fully assess the structural response of shield tunnels under different loading scenarios.

## Conclusions

This manuscript combines field investigations and model tests to study differential settlement in shield tunnels during operational period. The primary conclusions are as follows:

1. Engineering case studies indicate that a sudden increase in surface load alters the original stress equilibrium of the soil, causing differential deformation in the shield tunnel. This can ultimately lead to structural distress such as bolt fracture, concrete spalling, cracking, and crushing in the segments. Furthermore, longitudinal differential settlement of the shield tunnel can result in failure of the joint sealing gaskets. In areas with abundant groundwater, this primarily manifests as water leakage at the springline of the tunnel.
2. Compared to the scenario with no offset (S = 0D), the vertical earth pressure on the tunnel crown decreased by 50.1% and 75.3% at offset distances of (S = 1D) and (S = 2D), respectively. When the surface load is offset by a distance of (S = 2D), its influence on the longitudinal differential settlement of the shield tunnel is minimized, with a maximum crown settlement value of 1.85 mm. Therefore, during construction activities adjacent to existing shield tunnels, the offset distance between the ground load and the tunnel should exceed 2D.
3. Compared to the burial depth of (H = 1D), the vertical earth pressure on the tunnel crown decreased by 18.7% and 78.3% at burial depths of (H = 2D) and (H = 3D), respectively. This indicates that when the tunnel burial depth exceeds 3D, the earth pressure acting on the shield tunnel structure is significantly reduced due to the soil arching effect, thereby decreasing the differential settlement of the tunnel. The maximum settlement value was reduced by approximately 9.13 mm compared to the case with (H = 1D).
4. Compared to a homogeneous sand layer, the presence of a soft soil interlayer significantly increases the peak tunnel settlement by nearly 83.3% and raises the maximum differential settlement by nearly 8 mm. When the soft soil interlayer contains clay with a water content between 40% and 60%, the maximum crown settlement of the shield tunnel varies only slightly, ranging between 10 mm and 12 mm. These findings demonstrate that longitudinally varying soil strata significantly increase the risk of differential settlement in shield tunnels under surface loading.
