## Accounting Method and Indicators of Multilevel CO2 Emissions Based on Cost During Construction of Shield Tunnels

Shengdi Zheng 1,2 , Xiongyao Xie 1,2 and Biao Zhou 1,2, *

- 1 Key Laboratory of Geotechnical &amp; Underground Engineering, Ministry of Education, Tongji University, Shanghai 200092, China; 2232528@tongji.edu.cn (S.Z.); xiexiongyao@tongji.edu.cn (X.X.)
- 2 Department of Geotechnical Engineering, Tongji University, Shanghai 200092, China
* Correspondence: zhoubiao@tongji.edu.cn; Tel.: +86-21-65985210

Abstract: With the rapid expansion of shield tunnel projects in China, precise accounting of CO2 emissions throughout the entire process is essential for advancing green and low-carbon construction practices. This paper introduces an innovative CO2 accounting methodology utilizing a cost-based carbon emission coding system. It adopts a multilevel approach to carbon emission accounting, aligned with this coding system, which facilitates a detailed examination of carbon emission ratios and characteristics across various construction techniques. The analysis includes six typical shield tunnel projects varying in diameter, focusing on sub-projects to scrutinize CO2 emissions and establish specific indicators. The findings indicate that CO2 emissions per 10,000 yuan of investment, approximately 30.25% (or 3025 kgCO2e/10,000 yuan), are more consistent than those per unit of length. Moreover, the study highlights differing CO2 emission trends among sub-projects compared to whole tunnel projects, assessing emission indicators and distribution patterns in four sub-projects: shield excavation, segmental lining, internal structure, and tunnel reinforcement. From these findings, the paper suggests more precise and tailored strategies for CO2 reduction. This research provides a theoretical basis for future construction planning and carbon management strategies.

Keywords: CO2 emissions; shield tunnel; construction; carbon accounting method; indicators; project cost

## 1. Introduction

Achieving net-zero carbon emissions globally by 2050 poses a significant challenge. Various cross-industry initiatives have been mobilized in response to decentralization objectives [1]. China pledges to peak its CO2 emissions by 2030 [2]. To attain this goal, every sector is required to establish feasible CO2 reduction targets. The transportation network in China, the largest and fastest-growing worldwide, spans over six million kilometers. This network includes the most extensive systems of high-speed rail and highways globally, presenting substantial opportunities for CO2 reduction [3]. Although the construction sector in developed regions such as the US and Europe has reached peak carbon emissions, those from transportation continue to escalate. In this context, shield tunnels represent the most carbon-intensive mode of transport infrastructure [1,4], emitting more than 40 times the CO2 per unit of length compared to highways [5].

The construction industry, akin to the transportation sector, has advanced notably in CO2 emissions accounting. Owing to the high standardization across design, construction, and maintenance, along with precise accounting and thorough research [6-10], projects can be categorized into distinct structures for individual accounting. The methods and systems for CO2 emissions factor accounting are well-established [11,12] and bolstered by current standards and software.

This method incorporates the concept of indicators and performs calculations at the sub-project level. By providing specific CO2 emission indicators derived from six demonstration projects, this approach rectifies notable discrepancies in CO2 accounting and enhances the clarity of CO2 emission distribution within shield tunnels.

## 2. Accounting Method of Multilevel CO2 Emissions

To address the inadequacies of traditional CO2 emissions accounting methods, this study introduces a multilevel CO2 emissions accounting and coding system. As illustrated in Figure 1, It involves analyzing investment estimates, design calculations, construction plans, and other project specifics to gather data on material and machinery usage, project volumes, and investment figures. These data are then integrated with a localized CO2 emissions factor database and a cost factor database. The CO2 emissions coding system is utilized to calculate the carbon emissions for respective projects and to determine the associated CO2 emissions.

Figure 2 depicts the accounting process. Initially, project data are imported and processed. The purpose is to integrate these data into the CO2 emissions coding system to create a corresponding project coding database. This database is subsequently aligned with the carbon emission factor and cost factor databases. After determining the appropriate analysis levels and methods, the process culminates in the calculation of CO2 emissions distribution and the corresponding emission indicators.

In tunnel reinforcement, one effective strategy to reduce CO2 emissions involves increasing the tunnel length while simultaneously reducing the number of working shafts. This approach not only optimizes the construction process by minimizing the need for additional entry and exit points but also reduces the carbon footprint associated with the increasing the tunnel length while simultaneously reducing the number of working shafts.

This approach not only optimizes the construction process by minimizing the need for additional entry and exit points but also reduces the carbon footprint associated with the construction of each shaft. Additionally, efforts should focus on avoiding or minimizing crossings through challenging terrain, as such areas often require more energy-intensive construction methods and materials, leading to higher CO2 emissions. Decreasing the volume of secondary grouting, which is typically used to fill voids and stabilize the tunnel, can further contribute to emission reductions. Employing reinforcement methods that are known for their lower carbon output, such as advanced geosynthetic materials, can also play a key role in this context.

For internal structure projects, adopting designs that save on material usage, such as columnar structures, can significantly reduce wall usage. By diminishing the structural volume per unit of length, it is possible to enhance CO2 savings. These designs not only decrease the amount of concrete and steel required, both of which have high embodied carbon, but they also allow for more efficient use of space, potentially reducing the overall project footprint. It is important to tailor the most effective reinforcement strategies to the specific type of structure being constructed, ensuring that the chosen method provides the necessary strength and stability with the least environmental impact.

When it comes to shield segments, substituting traditional, high-emission steel segments with carbon-efficient alternatives, like those made from steel fiber, can substantially lower CO2 emissions. Steel fiber segments offer similar or even superior performance characteristics while having a smaller carbon footprint due to their production processes and the reduced need for additional support materials.

Shield excavation projects must carefully select synchronous grouting materials and work towards reducing the volumes of grout used. The choice of grouting material can greatly affect the overall emissions, as some types may require more energy to produce and apply. Efficient planning of soil transport routes to minimize the total distance traveled or using transportation methods that are more carbon-efficient (e.g., electric or hybrid vehicles) is also crucial. Moreover, maintaining an appropriately sized shield base is essential, as an oversized base can lead to unnecessary material usage and increased emissions during both the construction and operational phases of the tunnel.

## 5. Conclusions

To tackle the challenges and enhance the precision of CO2 emissions accounting in shield tunnels, this study introduces a Cost-based Multilevel CO2 Emissions Accounting Method. This method comprises a CO2 emissions coding system grounded in cost analysis and a corresponding accounting technique. It facilitates a comprehensive examination of CO2 emissions distribution within shield tunnels and elucidates the variations in emissions among different tunnels.

This study assessed CO2 emission distribution patterns in shield tunnels across six demonstration projects, and the average CO2 emissions of 15 m diameter shield tunnels is 86,706 kgCO2e/m. It revealed that as the diameter of the shield tunnel increases, the CO2 emissions per unit of length during the materialization phase rise accordingly, with a predominance of emissions originating from machinery over materials. In terms of overall tunnel metrics, CO2 emissions per 10,000 yuan of investment emerged as a more consistent indicator, registering an approximate value of 30.25% (or 3025 kgCO2e/10,000 yuan).

Furthermore, this study explored the sub-project level, developing tailored CO2 emissions estimation indicators for shield tunnels and their sub-projects. These indicators quantify CO2 emissions per unit of investment and per unit of length. The research also proposed specific carbon reduction measures for various sub-projects to address the considerable variations and ambiguous distributions of carbon emissions in shield tunnel accounting.

Future research should focus on calculating CO2 emissions across various projects to identify specific carbon reduction strategies. By integrating lower-carbon initiatives in the planning phase, it is possible to decrease CO2 emissions during the construction of shield tunnels.
