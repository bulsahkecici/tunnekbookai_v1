## Regional variation and prediction model of carbon emissions in the highway construction stage

Yubin Zhang 1,2 , Chunhong Wang 1,2 , Shouzhong Feng 3 , Qing Ma 4 , Weixing Mao 3 , Haiyan Xu 4  , Zhigang Wu 1,2 &amp; Dahai Yang 1,2

To reveal the regional differentiation characteristics of carbon emissions during the construction phase of expressways and to improve prediction accuracy, six typical expressway projects located in the plain, hilly, and mountainous regions of Anhui Province were selected as case studies. A carbon emission accounting model for the construction phase was established based on the life cycle assessment method, and the effects of the bridge-tunnel ratio, subproject structure, and material and energy consumption on carbon emission intensity were systematically analyzed. On this basis, a regional carbon emission prediction model was developed and optimized using data from 21 completed expressways across the province. The results indicate that carbon emission intensity exhibits a significant topographic gradient, with mountainous regions showing higher values than hilly regions, and hilly regions higher than plain regions. The maximum carbon emission intensity in mountainous projects reaches 5.27 × 10⁷ kg CO₂/km, which is 2.86 times that of plain regions. As terrain complexity increases, the carbon emission structure shifts from being dominated by subgrade engineering and interchange engineering to being dominated by structural engineering, such as bridges and tunnels. In mountainous regions, emissions from structural engineering account for more than 50% of the total emissions. At the material level, cement and steel are identified as the primary emission sources, jointly accounting for 78% of total emissions in mountainous projects, and demonstrating the highest sensitivity to variations in total emissions. The prediction results show that the baseline model using the bridge-tunnel ratio as a single variable achieves a coefficient of determination ( R² ) of 0.69. After incorporating material and energy consumption variables, the optimized XGBoost model improves the coefficient of determination to 0.9517, achieving highaccuracy prediction using only eight categories of material and energy consumption indicators. Based on the analytical results, differentiated emission reduction pathways are proposed. In mountainous regions, priority should be given to optimizing the design of tunnels and interchange engineering and controlling the intensity of high-carbon structural materials. In plain and hilly regions, emphasis should be placed on low-carbon design and construction optimization of bridge and culvert engineering and subgrade engineering. This study provides a data-driven basis for regional carbon emission prediction and emission reduction decision-making during the construction phase of expressways.

Keywords carbon emission quantification, Regional characteristics, Carbon emission prediction, Emission reduction measures, Modern transportation

The  continuous  expansion  of  the  global  transportation  industry  has  significantly  increased  carbon  dioxide (CO₂)  emissions  while  driving  economic  growth,  exacerbating  climate  change,  and  posing  challenges  to sustainable development. In response to climate change, China has set the goals of 'carbon peak by 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060' 1 .  The transportation, construction, and industrial sectors are the primary sources of CO₂ emissions, with the transportation sector accounting for about a quarter of global emissions, and this share is on the rise; road transport alone contributes 80% of transportation sector emissions 2 . Driven by policy and construction demands, the scale of expressways continues to expand, becoming a key area where both carbon emission pressure and reduction potential coexist in the transportation sector 3 . In  terms  of  carbon  accounting  methods,  a  relatively  mature  analytical  framework  has  been  established for expressway construction. Existing studies mainly utilize life cycle assessment (LCA) methods to quantify carbon emissions throughout the construction process 4 . Research has shown that the material production phase typically accounts for over 90% of carbon emissions during the construction phase 5,6 . Carbon emissions vary significantly across different types of engineering: the carbon emissions from the asphalt pavement construction phase can exceed 97% 7 ; bridge engineering has already developed a systematic evaluation framework 8,9 ; tunnel engineering, with its high carbon emission intensity, also presents material substitution reduction potential. For example, substituting cement and steel can reduce material production emissions by 1.83%-10.92% and 0.75%- 5.99%, respectively 10 .  Besides LCA, carbon footprint methods, emission factor methods, and greenhouse gas inventory methods have also been applied to decompose emissions during material production, transportation, and construction machinery operations 11 . Overall, existing studies clearly identify the key emission sources and structural characteristics in expressway construction. At the same time, the development of digital twins and smart construction technologies has significantly enhanced the digitalization of the construction process. Methods  based  on  artificial  neural  networks,  CNN-LSTM,  and  discrete  event  simulation  for  excavation prediction  and  construction  optimization  have  been  widely  applied 12-14 .  The  optimization  of  underground engineering  driven  by  digital  twins  has  been  validated  in  complex  geological  conditions 15 .  In  this  context, existing research has proposed a risk analysis method based on the general bowtie method to assess and manage risks associated with tunnel boring machines (TBM) under difficult ground conditions 16 . Furthermore, multicamera deep learning recognition technology has enabled equipment behavior monitoring during construction, particularly  under  occlusion  conditions.  Traditional  single-camera  methods  are  less  effective  under  these conditions.  Therefore,  adopting  multi-camera  and  dual-stream  CNN-LSTM  deep  learning  architectures  has significantly  improved  the  accuracy  of  excavator  activity  recognition 17 .  Multi-view  stereoscopic  vision  and point cloud registration methods have been used for earthwork volume estimation and real-time productivity assessment 18 .  Additionally, multimodal deep learning methods combining RGB images and point cloud data have demonstrated a recognition accuracy of 94.67% in field validations on various construction sites, providing effective  support  for  real-time  monitoring  integrated  into  digital  twin  systems 19 .  Despite  these  technologies performing exceptionally in construction efficiency and risk control, there remains a gap in the integration of carbon emission accounting during the construction phase and the use of smart technologies in emission reduction assessments.

In  addition  to  differences  in  engineering  types,  carbon  emissions  during  expressway  construction  also exhibit significant regional heterogeneity. Different topographic conditions, construction organization methods, and material structures lead to significant spatial differentiation in emission levels 20 . Taking Anhui Province as an example, the northern Anhui, Jianghuai, and southern Anhui mountainous regions display distinct spatial emission differentiation characteristics. Only through differentiated accounting can regional emission reduction potential be accurately identified and targeted pathways developed, and similar phenomena have been validated in other regions 21 .  Although methods such as scenario analysis and unit quota methods have been used for estimating emissions during the construction phase 22,23 ,  there is still a lack of a systematic research framework that covers the entire construction process, integrates data from multiple projects, and reflects regional differences.In recent years, low-carbon research in the built environment has expanded from individual accounting to regional system optimization and predictive modeling. Strategies such as ground-source heat pump sharing 24 , urban microclimate prediction models 25 ,  facade photovoltaic optimization 26 ,  thermochromic pavement materials 27 , and sponge city models based on the coupling of SWMM and LCA 28  all reflect the trend of moving from static accounting to scenario analysis and multi-objective collaborative optimization. However, compared with the building and municipal systems, there is still a lack of regionalized predictive models based on multi-project data for expressway construction, and the gradient differences in emission structures and emission reduction potential under different topographic conditions have not yet been systematically revealed.

Based  on  this,  this  study  constructs  a  carbon  emission  accounting  and  regionalized  prediction  model for  the  expressway  construction  phase  under  the  life  cycle  assessment  framework.  Six  typical  projects  from Anhui Province, representing the plain, hilly, and mountainous regions, are selected to analyze the subproject and material emission structures, revealing the emission gradient characteristics under different topographic conditions. Furthermore, data from 21 expressway projects in the province during the construction phase are integrated to establish a regionalized carbon emission prediction model, enabling differentiated assessments and proposing targeted emission reduction strategies. This research provides  methodological  support  for  carbon  emission  prediction  during  the  expressway  construction  phase and  regional  emission  reduction  decision-making,  and  offers  a  reference  for  improving  the  environmental performance throughout the life cycle of transportation infrastructure.

## Materials and methods Carbon emission accounting method

Accounting method and scope

The life cycle assessment (LCA) method has been widely adopted in carbon emission quantification studies both  domestically  and  internationally,  and  serves  as  a  core  analytical  tool  for  identifying  emission  sources, quantifying carbon footprints, and formulating emission reduction strategies. LCA emphasizes comprehensive accounting of resource consumption and emissions throughout a defined stage under clearly specified system boundaries. Accordingly, this study conducts a systematic accounting and analysis of carbon emissions during the construction phase of expressways within the LCA theoretical framework.

## Case analysis of highway projects

Basic information of the highways

This study takes 21 completed expressways in Anhui Province as the overall sample, with construction periods spanning from 2014 to 2024. The engineering data were primarily obtained from construction drawing budget documents, bills of quantities, and final settlement reports. To ensure data consistency and reliability, the budget estimates and final settlement data were cross-checked item by item, with the overall discrepancy controlled within 5%. For a small proportion of missing or incomplete data, the mean value correction method based on similar types of engineering projects was adopted. All data revision records were fully retained to ensure the traceability and reproducibility of the research results.

On this basis, six representative projects were selected from three typical topographic regions for in-depth analysis, namely Projects A, B, C, D, E, and F. These projects correspond to the Huaibei Plain (A and B), the Hilly Region (C and D), and the Southern Anhui Mountainous Region (E and F), respectively. The selected projects exhibit significant differences in route length, relatively similar construction periods, and typical topographic characteristics, which provide favorable conditions for regional comparison and structural difference analysis.

## Sub-project and material carbon emission analysis

This study systematically analyzes the carbon emissions of eight subproject engineering categories for the six selected expressway projects, including temporary engineering, subgrade engineering, pavement engineering, bridge and culvert engineering, tunnel engineering, interchange engineering, traffic engineering and ancillary facilities,  and  landscaping  and  environmental  protection  engineering.  In  addition,  the  carbon  emissions  of eight major material categories were examined, including cement, steel, energy, asphalt, aggregates, lime, and other materials. The data were obtained from construction design drawings and the bills of quantities for each project. These data were cross-checked against the final settlement reports. For any discrepancies identified, summation and averaging procedures were applied to reconcile the differences, thereby ensuring data accuracy and consistency.

## Results

## Impact of bridge-tunnel ratio on carbon emission intensity

As shown in Fig. 2(a), significant differences in the bridge-tunnel ratio (i.e., the proportion of the total length of bridges and tunnels to the total route length) are observed among expressways in different topographic regions. Projects A and B are located in the plain region, with bridge-tunnel ratios of 8% and 5%, respectively, both below 10%. These projects are dominated by bridge and culvert engineering, with no tunnel engineering constructed. Projects C and D are situated in the hilly region, with bridge-tunnel ratios of 16% and 19%, respectively, falling within the range of 15%-20%. Projects E and F are located in the mountainous region, where the bridge-tunnel ratios reach 72% and 77%, respectively, and tunnel engineering constitutes the dominant structural form. These results indicate that increasing terrain complexity significantly alters the route structural configuration, thereby affecting the composition of engineering works.

In terms of carbon emissions, Fig. 2(b) shows that carbon emission intensity exhibits a gradient distribution pattern of 'mountainous region &gt; hilly region &gt; plain region, ' with error bars controlled within ± 5%. Although Project A has a relatively high total carbon emissions, its carbon emission intensity per unit length remains comparatively low due to its longer route length. Projects C and D demonstrate moderate levels of both total carbon emissions and carbon emission intensity. In contrast, Projects E and F, despite having shorter route lengths, exhibit substantially higher carbon emission intensity per unit length. This is primarily attributable to the high proportion of bridge and tunnel engineering, which significantly increases the demand for energyintensive materials and construction energy, thereby serving as the main driver of carbon emission growth.

To quantify the effect of the bridge-tunnel ratio on project-level carbon emission intensity, the relationship between the bridge-tunnel ratio and carbon emission intensity was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3, the bridgetunnel ratio ranges from 2% to 77%, corresponding to a carbon emission intensity of 1.84 × 10⁷-5.27 × 10⁷ kg CO₂/km. Correlation analysis indicates a significant positive relationship between the bridge-tunnel ratio and carbon emission intensity, with a Pearson correlation coefficient ( r ) of 0.829, significant at the 0.001 level. The coefficient of determination ( R² ) of the linear regression model is 0.6877, while that of the quadratic polynomial regression model is 0.6899. The two models exhibit comparable goodness of fit and effectively capture the overall increasing trend of carbon emission intensity with the rise in the bridge-tunnel ratio. The regression results demonstrate that carbon emission intensity increases significantly as the bridge-tunnel ratio increases. When the bridge-tunnel ratio exceeds approximately 0.6, the growth rate of the quadratic regression curve becomes slightly higher than that of the linear model, indicating a potential acceleration effect of carbon emission intensity in the high bridge-tunnel ratio range. These findings suggest that bridge and tunnel engineering exhibit relatively high carbon emission intensity in terms of material input and construction energy consumption, providing a quantitative basis for route alignment optimization and bridge-tunnel ratio control in expressway planning and design.

## Analysis of material-related carbon emissions

To reveal the differences in material-related carbon emissions during the construction phase of expressways across  different  regions,  a  systematic  analysis  of  material  emission  characteristics  was  conducted  for  three representative topographic categories. Based on the previously established carbon emission accounting model and  the  summarized  labor-material-machinery  data  from  the  construction  drawing  budget  documents,  a complete  material  inventory  was  compiled.  The  actual  quantities  of  each  material  were  then  matched  with the  corresponding carbon emission factors for conversion, enabling the calculation of total material-related carbon emissions for each project. Finally, the results were aggregated by subproject engineering category and comparatively analyzed in graphical form. Figure 6 illustrates the composition characteristics of major materialrelated carbon emissions during the construction phase for Projects A and B in the plain region, Projects C and D in the hilly region, and Projects E and F in the mountainous region from two dimensions. Figure 6a presents a stacked area chart, reflecting the variation trend of material emission proportions with increasing terrain complexity. Figure 6b presents a stacked bar chart, quantitatively displaying the proportional structure of material-related carbon emissions for each project, thereby revealing the influence of topographic conditions on the composition of emission sources.

The results indicate that cement and steel consistently constitute the primary emission sources across all project types, and their proportions increase significantly with rising terrain complexity. In the mountainous projects, the proportion of cement-related emissions reaches 40% for Project E and 39% for Project F, markedly higher than 26% for Project A and 24% for Project B in the plain region. For steel, Project E exhibits the highest proportion among all samples, reaching 38%, indicating that mountainous projects, due to higher bridge-tunnel ratios  and  increased structural complexity, show significantly greater dependence on high-strength concrete and reinforcing steel. Meanwhile, the proportion of energy-related emissions increases in mountainous projects, reflecting the driving effect of high-power construction equipment and long-distance material transportation on energy consumption. In contrast, relatively low-emission materials such as aggregates and lime exhibit declining proportions in mountainous projects. In particular, the proportions of lime and asphalt in Projects E and F are close to zero, whereas they remain at approximately 1% in Projects A, B, C, and D. Additionally, the proportion of 'other materials' in Project F reaches 24%, which may be attributed to the adoption of special structural forms, increased use of auxiliary materials under complex geological conditions, or differences in construction techniques.

Overall, Fig. 6 demonstrates that as terrain transitions from plain to mountainous regions, the carbon emission structure during the construction phase of expressways becomes increasingly concentrated in high-emission elements such as cement, steel, and energy. This structural shift reflects the high-emission characteristics of mountainous projects in terms of structural design, material allocation, and construction organization. Therefore, expressway construction in mountainous regions should prioritize structural optimization, substitution with low-carbon  materials,  and  the  application  of  energy-saving  construction  technologies.  Particular  attention should be given to controlling the use intensity of high-emission materials and exploring material reduction and substitution pathways, thereby providing technical support for green transportation infrastructure development under complex terrain conditions.

## Discussion Prediction method and validation

Initial model selection

The sample of this study consists of 21 completed expressway projects in Anhui Province, with construction periods  spanning  from  2014  to  2022.  The  original  data  were  obtained  from  construction  drawing  budget documents, bills of quantities, and final settlement reports. To ensure data consistency, cross-verification was conducted between the budget estimates and final settlement data, with discrepancies controlled within 5%. For a small number of missing values, the mean value correction method based on similar types of engineering projects and the standard carbon emission factor supplementation method were applied. All data revision records were retained to ensure traceability and reproducibility. To evaluate the out-of-sample predictive performance of the model, a stratified hold-out approach was adopted. One representative expressway was selected from each of the three topographic categories-plain, hilly, and mountainous regions-as external validation samples, while the remaining 18 projects were used as the training set. The validation samples were completely excluded during the model fitting stage to prevent data leakage. Subsequently, the statistical relationship between the bridgetunnel ratio and carbon emission intensity per kilometer during the construction phase was re-fitted using the remaining 18 expressways. The reserved validation samples were then employed for extrapolation prediction to assess the stability and generalization capability of the model on unseen data. The actual carbon emissions of the three reserved validation samples are presented in Table 2.

Therefore, in the initial prediction stage, both linear regression and quadratic regression models were employed in parallel, and their predictive performances were compared to evaluate their applicability across different bridge-tunnel ratio intervals.

## Initial model validation

Based on the extrapolation results of the three stratified hold-out validation samples, the out-of-sample prediction errors of the linear regression model are MAE = 5.35 × 10⁶kg/km, RMSE = 6.04 × 10⁶kg/km, and MAPE = 20.97%. The results indicate that using only the bridge-tunnel ratio as the independent variable yields an out-ofsample relative error of approximately 21%, with a coefficient of determination ( R² ) of 0.69. This suggests that the bridge-tunnel ratio is capable of capturing, at a macroscopic level, the overall influence of route structural configuration  on  carbon  emissions  during  the  construction  phase.  However,  the  predictive  accuracy  of  the single-variable model remains limited, particularly in the high bridge-tunnel ratio range and under compounded topographic differences, where error convergence is insufficient. The primary reason is that the bridge-tunnel ratio cannot explicitly reflect inter-project variations in key material consumption and energy use structure. These factors constitute a substantial proportion of carbon emissions during the construction phase and vary significantly with changes in engineering structure and topographic conditions.

Therefore, in the subsequent model optimization, this study further incorporates major carbon emission sources-namely steel, cement, lime, and energy consumption-as covariates in addition to the bridge-tunnel ratio, and constructs a multivariate regression prediction model. This approach aims to achieve higher fitting accuracy and stronger out-of-sample generalization capability under different topographic conditions.

## (2) XGBoost Regression Model.

The traditional  Ridge  Regression  model  typically  relies  on  linear  combinations  of  input  features,  and  its optimization  objective  is  to  reduce  fluctuations  in  regression  coefficients  through  regularization,  thereby mitigating the influence of multicollinearity. Although Ridge Regression can effectively alleviate multicollinearity via L 2 regularization, it is inherently limited to modeling linear relationships and cannot adequately capture interaction  effects  among  features. During model training, each tree is optimized according to the residuals of the target variable, and the final prediction is obtained through a weighted aggregation of all trees. ## Regional project prediction application

The proposed prediction model is not only applicable to general carbon emission estimation but is particularly suitable  for  regionalized  project-level  carbon  emission  prediction.  Under  different  topographic  conditions, material  consumption  patterns  and  energy  use  structures  vary  considerably  across  projects.  Therefore,  the model explicitly accounts for regional characteristics during the prediction process. Through the stratified holdout validation approach, the stability and generalization capability of the model under different terrain types were ensured.

In  regional  project  prediction,  the  model  estimates  carbon  emissions  based  on  region-specific  material consumption,  energy  consumption,  and  structural  characteristics.  The  XGBoost  model,  by  incorporating multiple  covariates  and  relying  primarily  on  material  consumption  and  energy  consumption  variables  for prediction, avoids overdependence on the bridge-tunnel ratio as a repeated explanatory variable. Even when limited to eight categories of material and energy consumption indicators, the model achieves high prediction accuracy across different project types. The key advantage of regionalized prediction lies in its ability to provide quantitative carbon emission assessments tailored to specific geographic regions and engineering structures, thereby assisting decision-makers in formulating targeted low-carbon design strategies during the planning and implementation stages.

The optimized XGBoost model offers more precise carbon emission estimation for projects located in diverse topographic regions, supporting the development of sustainable infrastructure. By enabling effective carbon emission prediction at the early design stage, the model provides robust data support for regional planning and green design optimization, thereby promoting the implementation of environmentally responsible construction practices.

## Targeted energy-saving and carbon reduction measures

Based  on  the  above  findings,  the  following  conclusions  can  be  drawn.  In  mountainous  regions,  particular attention should be paid to carbon emissions from interchange engineering and tunnel engineering, whereas in hilly regions, priority should be given to carbon emissions from bridge and culvert engineering. From the material perspective, cement constitutes the largest contributor to carbon emissions in mountainous regions, while steel represents the dominant emission source in both mountainous and hilly regions. Accordingly, this study proposes targeted carbon emission reduction measures for bridge and culvert engineering and tunnel engineering,  and  further  advances  carbon  mitigation  strategies  for  cement  and  steel  during  the  material production stage, with the objective of reducing carbon emissions throughout the infrastructure construction process.  Carbon  emission  reduction  can  be  achieved  through  four  principal  pathways:  carbon  substitution, carbon reduction, carbon sequestration, and carbon control.

## Mitigation measures for tunnel works

Carbon emission mitigation strategies for tunnel engineering primarily encompass five aspects: site selection, structural design, portal design, land conservation, and construction management. During the site selection stage,  large-scale  carbon  sink  areas  should  be  avoided  to  preserve  carbon  sequestration  capacity.  When necessary, route optimization, vegetation relocation, and ecological restoration measures can be implemented to  minimize  carbon  loss.  In  structural  design,  emission  reduction  can  be  achieved  by  optimizing  crosssectional configurations, promoting prefabricated structures, and reducing raw material consumption. Portal design should conform to natural terrain conditions to reduce slope engineering quantities, while integrating vegetation restoration and landscape reshaping to balance structural safety and carbon mitigation objectives. During the construction and operation stages, priority should be given to clean energy sources and energyefficient equipment to improve overall energy efficiency.

Regarding construction methods, the shield tunneling method exhibits lower carbon emissions compared with the drill-and-blast method, with its unit construction carbon emission intensity being approximately 50% of the latter 29 . The combination of shield tunneling and prefabrication technology yields significant emission reduction effects.  For instance, prefabricated construction of internal support systems can reduce carbon emissions by 7% 30 ; integrated permanent-temporary prefabricated construction can achieve approximately 15% reduction 31 ; and the dual-tunnel combined pipe-jacking and underground excavation method can reduce emissions by about 11% 32 . It is noteworthy that carbon emissions during shield tunnel construction are primarily concentrated in the building material production stage, particularly during prefabricated segment production, which accounts for 72.73% of total emissions, mainly originating from cement and steel consumption 33 . Therefore, optimizing material selection and improving construction processes are critical for further emission reduction.

## Mitigation measures for bridge and culvert works

Bridge  and  culvert engineering  mainly  consists  of foundations,  substructures, superstructures, bridge deck  pavement,  and  ancillary  facilities.  Life  cycle  carbon  emissions  are  predominantly  concentrated  in the material production stage, accounting for approximately 83% of the total, mainly arising from steel and cement production 34 . Therefore, the rational selection of green and low-carbon materials constitutes the core pathway for  emission  reduction.  High-performance  concrete  (HPC),  by  reducing  cement  consumption  and enhancing durability, can effectively decrease carbon emissions during both the construction phase and the entire life cycle. Lightweight concrete (LWC) reduces structural self-weight, thereby lowering seismic effects and reducing reinforcement and pile foundation quantities, achieving both carbon reduction and structural optimization benefits. The use of recycled reinforcing steel also demonstrates substantial mitigation potential. Its carbon emission factor is only 0.377 kg CO₂/kg, representing a 91.73% reduction compared with conventional reinforcing steel, and can achieve up to a 73% reduction in carbon emissions for a single bridge project 35 .

In addition to material selection, construction methods significantly influence carbon emissions. Compared with  the  full  falsework  method,  the  incremental  launching  method  achieves  superior  carbon  reduction performance due to reduced scaffold and concrete consumption. In composite box girder bridges, replacing conventional concrete with high-performance concrete can reduce carbon emissions by 23.3% under the castin-place method and by 22.7% under the incremental launching method 36 . Overall, the synergistic application of green materials and optimized construction methods can substantially reduce carbon emissions in bridge and culvert engineering, demonstrating strong practical value for engineering implementation.

## Cement reduction strategies

Cement  emission  mitigation  strategies  focus  on  two  primary  aspects:  reducing  cement  consumption  and decreasing carbon emissions during cement production. Carbon emissions from cement production mainly originate  from  the  clinker  production  stage,  including  carbonate  decomposition  and  fuel  combustion. Fuel  combustion  accounts  for  approximately  30%  of  total  emissions,  whereas  CO₂  released  from  carbonate decomposition exceeds 60%, constituting the principal carbon source in cement production 37 . To address fuel combustion emissions, improving energy efficiency and optimizing the energy structure are effective mitigation measures. Replacing traditional fossil fuels with biomass fuels, refuse-derived fuel (RDF), or other renewable energy  sources  can  significantly  reduce  combustion-related  emissions.  Regarding  carbonate  decomposition, carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology represents a critical mitigation pathway. By capturing and storing CO₂ generated during decomposition, emissions to the atmosphere can be effectively reduced.

In  terms  of  material  reduction,  decreasing  cement  content  is  also  an  important  emission  mitigation strategy.  Common  approaches  include  partially  substituting  cement  clinker  with  mineral  admixtures  such as  limestone  powder,  fly  ash,  slag,  and  waste  glass  powder,  thereby  significantly  reducing  carbon  emissions per  unit  of  concrete.  Furthermore,  the  promotion  of  low-carbon  concrete  and  novel  cementitious  materials provides substantial mitigation potential. For example, low-carbon concrete can reduce carbon emissions by more than 70% compared with conventional concrete. Ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC), due to its superior mechanical properties and durability, can significantly reduce material usage while satisfying structural performance requirements, thereby achieving both emission reduction and enhanced engineering performance [42]. In summary, improving energy efficiency, advancing CCUS technologies, reducing clinker content, and promoting  high-performance,  low-carbon  cementitious  materials  collectively  form  an  effective  synergistic pathway for emission reduction in cement production and application.

## Steel reduction strategies

Compared with cast-in-place concrete structures,  steel  structures  can  reduce  life  cycle  carbon  emissions  by approximately 15%-45% 38 , indicating considerable mitigation potential. The steel manufacturing process mainly includes cutting, welding and assembly, surface treatment, and coating. Carbon emissions arise primarily from fossil fuel combustion (e.g., coal and coke), emissions during component processing, embodied emissions in auxiliary materials, and purchased electricity consumption 39 .  Accordingly, steel emission mitigation strategies can be addressed from three perspectives: energy structure optimization, manufacturing process improvement, and material performance enhancement. Optimizing the energy structure by introducing low-carbon fossil energy or renewable energy sources (e.g., photovoltaic power and wind energy) to partially replace conventional energy sources can effectively  reduce  emissions  during  steel  processing  and  electricity  consumption  stages.  Process improvements, such as adopting laser cutting, integrated welding and correction technologies, high-efficiency welding, and laser rust removal, can significantly reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions during each manufacturing stage while simultaneously enhancing production quality and efficiency. The adoption of high-performance steel is also a critical mitigation strategy. High-strength steel can reduce steel consumption while meeting structural safety and durability requirements. For instance, promoting the use of high-strength reinforcing steel with strength grades of 500 MPa and above can reduce steel consumption by more than 10% under  equivalent  structural  performance  conditions 40 ,  thereby  indirectly  lowering  carbon  emissions  during material production and transportation stages. The synergistic integration of energy optimization, technological upgrading, and high-performance material application can substantially enhance carbon emission mitigation performance in steel production, offering significant potential for widespread engineering implementation.

## Regional emission reduction measures

Based on the aforementioned carbon emission analysis results, the implementation of regional carbon emission reduction measures for expressway construction under different terrain conditions should fully consider the specific characteristics and engineering demands of each region. These findings not only provide quantitative evidence for policymakers in formulating mitigation strategies but also offer practical guidance for engineers during  the  design  and  construction  stages.  The  differentiated  emission  reduction  measures  and  application recommendations based on topographic conditions are summarized as follows.

(1) Emission Reduction Strategies for Mountainous Projects.

Mountainous projects are typically characterized by high bridge-tunnel ratios, resulting in tunnel engineering and interchange engineering becoming the dominant carbon emission sources. Accordingly, mitigation strategies should  focus  on  reducing  emissions  from  these  two  engineering  categories.  During  the  site  selection  stage, project alignment should avoid carbon sink areas as much as possible. Route optimization, vegetation relocation, and ecological restoration measures should be adopted to minimize carbon loss. During the construction stage, low-carbon  structural  design  should  be  prioritized,  and  low-emission  shield  tunneling  methods  should  be promoted. Compared with the conventional drill-and-blast method, the shield tunneling method can reduce carbon emission intensity by approximately 50%. When combined with prefabricated construction technologies, additional emission reductions can be achieved during the construction phase. In terms of material selection, mountainous projects exhibit strong dependence on steel and cement, particularly in tunnel engineering and bridge engineering. Therefore, high-performance concrete (HPC) and high-strength steel (e.g., 500 MPa grade reinforcing steel) should be preferentially adopted. The promotion of recycled reinforcing steel and renewable material substitution for conventional steel can further significantly reduce carbon emissions. Additionally, in cement production, the implementation of clinker reduction strategies can simultaneously reduce production costs and decrease carbon emissions during the manufacturing process, thereby optimizing the carbon emission intensity of materials.

(2) Emission Reduction Strategies for Plain and Hilly Projects.

In plain and hilly regions, bridge and culvert engineering constitutes the primary source of carbon emissions, particularly due to steel and cement production. Therefore, the selection of green and low-carbon materials at the design stage is crucial. High-performance concrete (HPC) is recommended, as it reduces cement consumption while enhancing structural durability, thereby lowering life cycle carbon emissions. Lightweight concrete (LWC) also  demonstrates  superior  emission  reduction  performance  by  reducing  structural  self-weight,  decreasing reinforcement and pile foundation demand, and achieving both structural optimization and carbon mitigation benefits. Optimization of construction methods is equally important. The terrain complexity in hilly regions necessitates environmentally friendly construction approaches, such as the cast-in-place method with optimized formwork  systems  and  the  incremental  launching  method.  These  approaches  reduce  scaffold  and  concrete consumption, thereby lowering construction-stage carbon emissions. Given regional terrain differences, plain and hilly projects should adopt targeted material optimization and construction techniques to reduce the carbon emission burden associated with bridge and culvert engineering and subgrade engineering.

(3) Policy Implications and Practical Applications.

At the planning stage, governmental authorities should incorporate carbon emission assessment indicators into  transportation  infrastructure  project  approval  procedures  and  require  low-carbon  design  evaluations during  the  design  phase.  Policy  instruments  supporting  the  application  of  low-carbon  technologies  should be  established,  and  financial  incentives  or  subsidies  should  be  provided  for  projects  adopting  green  design, low-carbon materials, and energy optimization technologies. Such measures would effectively accelerate the widespread  adoption  of  low-carbon  technologies  and  promote  sustainable  transportation  infrastructure development. In practical implementation, projects should formulate customized carbon emission reduction measures according to terrain characteristics. For example, mountainous projects should prioritize emission mitigation in tunnel engineering and interchange engineering, with emphasis on optimizing steel and cement consumption. In contrast, plain and hilly regions should focus on low-carbon construction methods for bridge and culvert engineering and subgrade engineering. With ongoing technological advancements, future projects should further promote the application of low-carbon construction materials, recycled materials, and intelligent construction equipment to enhance overall carbon efficiency. At the policy level, governments should refine emission control measures targeting high-carbon materials such as steel and cement, promote standardized application of green construction materials, and improve carbon trading mechanisms to facilitate the transition of the transportation infrastructure sector toward a low-carbon and sustainable development pathway.

## Economic feasibility and cost-benefit analysis

In  the  proposed  regional  emission  reduction  measures,  economic feasibility  is  a  key  factor  determining  the effectiveness of implementation. Taking mountainous projects as an example, while the use of high-performance concrete  (HPC)  and  high-strength  steel  (500  MPa  grade  reinforcing  steel)  can  significantly  reduce  carbon emissions, the initial cost of these materials is relatively high. Therefore, it is important to consider the long-term benefits of these materials. For example, HPC's superior durability reduces the need for frequent maintenance and replacement, which increases long-term economic benefits. Similarly, using recycled reinforcing steel and low-carbon substitute materials not only reduces material costs but also cuts transportation expenses, which is particularly important in mountainous projects. Moreover, the choice of construction methods also impacts cost-effectiveness. Although shield tunneling and incremental launching methods have higher initial investment costs, their construction efficiency and low carbon emission characteristics help reduce energy consumption and construction duration, ultimately lowering the overall project cost. Therefore, these methods offer higher economic returns over the long term. Lastly, government policy support is crucial for the economic feasibility of emission reduction measures. Fiscal subsidies, tax incentives, and green financing policies can effectively lower the initial costs of low-carbon technologies and promote their widespread adoption. In summary, by considering the  cost-effectiveness  of  emission  reduction  measures  and  making  decisions  based  on  long-term  economic returns, the widespread application of low-carbon technologies can be encouraged, ensuring the sustainable development of projects.

## Limitations and outlook

Although this study has made significant progress in predicting carbon emissions during the construction phase, there are several limitations that require further exploration in future research. First, while carbon emissions during the construction phase contribute significantly to overall emissions, emissions during the operation and demolition phases also play a crucial role. Future research should expand to include these phases, offering a more comprehensive carbon emission assessment to enhance the accuracy and completeness of carbon emission predictions. Second, this study did not analyze the potential impact of design geometric parameters (such as span width, excavation depth, foundation layout, etc.) on carbon emissions. Geometric parameters during the design phase can significantly influence material consumption and energy usage, which directly affects carbon emissions.  Therefore,  future  research  should  investigate  the  influence  of  these  design  variables  on  carbon emissions to further refine carbon emission prediction models.

## Conclusions

This study established a carbon emission accounting model for the construction phase of expressways based on the life cycle assessment (LCA) method. Six typical expressway projects from Anhui Province, representing three  topographic  regions-plain,  hilly,  and  mountainous-were  selected  as  the  study  objects.  The  study systematically  analyzed  the  impact  of  bridge-tunnel  ratio,  subproject  structure,  and  material  use  on  carbon emission intensity, and developed a carbon emission prediction model suitable for multiple regional scenarios. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Significant Regional Differences in Carbon Emission Characteristics: The carbon emission intensity per kilometer for mountainous projects reached a maximum of 5.27 × 10⁷ kg CO₂/km, which is 2.86 times higher than the 1.84 × 10⁷ kg CO₂/km in the plain region. This difference is closely related to the higher bridge-tunnel ratio  and  the  larger  proportion  of  tunnel  and  interchange  engineering.  As  terrain  complexity  increases,  the carbon emission structure shifts from being dominated by subgrade and interchange engineering (approximately 70%) in the plain region to being dominated by structural engineering, such as bridges and tunnels, in the mountainous region (over 50%). This reflects the significant impact of topographic conditions on engineering structure and emission composition.

(2) Clear Identification of Major Emission Sources and Reduction Potential: Cement and steel are the two main sources of carbon emissions during the construction phase. In mountainous projects, they together account for 78% of total emissions, with cement contributing 40% and steel 38%. Sensitivity analysis shows that changes in the cement emission factor have the greatest impact on total emissions, with a fluctuation of up to ± 15%, followed by steel at ± 13%. Energy-related materials exhibit increased sensitivity in mountainous projects, while lime's influence is more pronounced in the plain and hilly regions, with a sensitivity of around ± 10%. Based on these findings, mountainous projects should prioritize optimizing tunnel and interchange engineering designs and controlling the use of high-carbon structural materials. In the plain and hilly regions, efforts should focus on low-carbon design and construction optimization for bridges and subgrade engineering.

(3) Development and Optimization of Multi-Level Carbon Emission Prediction Models: The basic model using only the bridge-tunnel ratio as a variable achieved an R 2 of  0.69.  By  introducing  material  and  energy consumption  variables,  ridge  regression  and  XGBoost  regression  models  were  developed.  The  optimized XGBoost model improved the coefficient of determination (R 2 ) to 0.9517, significantly enhancing prediction accuracy and explanatory power. The model achieved high-precision prediction of project carbon emissions using just 8 categories of material and energy consumption indicators.

(4) Proposed Regional Differentiated Mitigation Technology Paths: Mountainous projects should prioritize shield  tunneling,  which  reduces  carbon  emissions  by  about  50%  compared to the traditional drill-and-blast method. Additionally, the application of recycled reinforcing steel can reduce emissions by up to 73%. In the plain and hilly regions, the promotion of high-performance concrete technology can reduce carbon emissions by approximately 23.3%, while the adoption of incremental launching construction methods can reduce emissions by about 22.7%. These technical pathways provide targeted solutions for low-carbon construction under different topographic conditions.

(5)  Strengthening  Policy  and  Practical  Integration:  It  is  recommended  to  incorporate  carbon  emission assessment mechanisms during the project planning stage, integrating low-carbon design and green material selection into the decision-making process. Policies and financial incentives should be implemented to promote the  application  of  low-carbon  technologies.  These  measures  will  help  achieve  carbon  reduction  goals  while ensuring the economic viability and feasibility of the projects.
