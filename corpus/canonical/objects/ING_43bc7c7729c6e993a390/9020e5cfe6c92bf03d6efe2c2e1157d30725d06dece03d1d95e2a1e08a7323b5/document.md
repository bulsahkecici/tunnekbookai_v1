## [Value management implementation for improving sustainability in construction projects: integrating life cycle cost and weighted evaluation analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1780599/full)

Ahmad M. Zamil 1 *, Mohammad Alhusban 2 and Abdullah Alharkan 3

1 Department of Marketing, College of Business Administration, Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University, Al Kharj, Saudi Arabia, 2 Department of Civil Engineering, Middle East University, Amman, Jordan, 3 College of Business Administration, Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University, Al Kharj, Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Value  management  (VM)  has  demonstrated  effectiveness  in construction projects, yet remains underutilized in tunneling. This study addresses the gap by applying VM systematically to a major tunneling project in Saudi Arabia to improve sustainability and long-term value.

Methods: A  structured  case-study  design  involving  12  multidisciplinary experts was adopted. The VM process integrated six standard phases with function  analysis,  weighted  multi-criteria  evaluation,  and  Life  Cycle  Cost (LCC) analysis.

Results: The preferred alternative achieved a cost reduction of SAR 6,869,411 while improving sustainability-related performance. VM combined with sustainability criteria and LCC analysis identified cost-effective alternatives.

Discussion: The  study  contributes  a  replicable  early-stage  decision-support framework for underground infrastructure projects.

## KEYWORDS

life cycle costing, multi-criteria evaluation, sustainability assessment, tunnelling projects, underground infrastructure, value management

## 1 Introduction

Value management (VM) is a structured, systematic technique used to maximise the value  of  a  project  by  realising  required  functions  at  the  lowest  possible  cost  without compromising  quality,  performance  or  sustainability  (Venkataraman  and  Pinto,  2023; Kineber et al., 2022f).  Organising from  value engineering,  VM  goes  beyond  cost reduction  to  focus  on  balancing  function,  cost  and  long-term  benefits.  The  process  is typically  organised  into  six  phases-information  analysis,  evaluation,  development,  and presentation-through  which  multidisciplinary  teams  collaboratively  identify  and  evaluate alternative solutions. VM is not solely a cost-cutting tool but a decision-making framework that embeds sustainability principles such as resource efficiency, environmental protection, life cycle cost reduction and social wellbeing (Kineber et al., 2022f; Jadidoleslami and Azizi, 2022). By doing so, VM improves project outcomes, ensures stakeholder satisfaction, and supports  the  delivery  of  resilient  infrastructure.  Its  application  in  construction  and infrastructure  projects,  such  as  tunnelling,  illustrates  its  potential  to  optimise  resource use, minimise  risks and  align technical solutions with  wider economic, environmental and social objectives.  The rapid  growth of urban areas and the expansion of cities have made  the construction  industry  a  vital  sector  for  meeting  societal  needs and  enhancing  the  quality  of  life  (Elseknidy  et  al.,  2025a).  The construction industry is integral to improving the quality of human life,  with  its  ongoing  advancements  in  infrastructure  (Nasereddin and Price, 2021; Al-Mekhlafi et al., 2023; Kineber et al., 2022a). In this context, and as the global construction sector increasingly shifts toward sustainability, green buildings (GBs) have emerged as a key solution  for  reducing  environmental  impact  (Elseknidy  et  al., 2025b).  Tunnels,  in  particular,  are  key  elements  of  transportation networks,  ensuring  efficient  transport  systems  and  public  safety (Tušer and Hošková-Mayerová, 2023; Haiko et al., 2025). To this end, government  agencies  continually  strive to  maintain  and enhance  tunnel  infrastructure.  Given  their  critical  importance, ensuring high-quality tunnel construction is paramount, providing  a  solid  foundation  for  informed  decision-making  in transportation  systems  (Zyen  and  Mulyono,  2024;  Afrazi  et  al., 2025). At the same time, recent construction sustainability research has emphasised that improving material utilisation, reducing waste, and lowering greenhouse gas emissions are central to advancing more sustainable built-environment practices,  reinforcing  the  importance  of  evaluation  frameworks that consider  environmental  performance alongside  economic value  (Oladunni  et  al.,  2025).  In  addition,  the  environmental burden  associated  with  transport-related  emissions  and  fossilfuel-dependent  infrastructure  systems  has  increased  the  urgency of  adopting  planning  and  design  approaches  that  support  carbon mitigation  and  sustainability-oriented  decision-making  (Oladunni and Olanrewaju, 2022; Oladunni et al., 2024). Sustainable construction aims to minimise the negative environmental impacts  of  buildings  by  enhancing  energy  efficiency,  encouraging the use of sustainable and recyclable materials, and reducing waste across the entire lifecycle of a building (Kineber et al., 2024b). To support the achievement of these goals, Value Management can play a significant role by providing a structured approach for evaluating alternatives that balance environmental, functional, and economic considerations (Ali et al., 2026). While VM has gained prominence across various infrastructure types, its focused application in tunnelling-particularly when combined with sustainability evaluation-remains  underdeveloped.  Over  the  past  3  decades, the  construction  and  engineering  industry  has  widely  adopted value  engineering  (VE),  now  recognised  as  value  management (VM).  VM  is  a  systematic  approach  that  enhances  a  product's value  by  eliminating  non-value-added  costs  (Ahmad  et  al.,  2022; Dara et al., 2025). This structured methodology has improved the quality of the project and maximised value for social development. Effective  decision-making  tools  are  essential  for  project  owners/ clients to select feasible, cost-effective, and sustainable options that would  ensure  sustainability  and  maximise  profitability.  A  notable example is the approval of approximately 800 VM recommendations for federal transportation infrastructure projects in 2019, yielding 10% cost savings ($3.1 billion) from the total project value of $32 billion. Federal law mandates VM studies for National Highway  System  and  bridge projects exceeding $50  million  and  $40  million,  respectively.  This  requirement  aims to incentivise the Departments of Transportation (DOTs) to adopt VM  practices,  maximising  value  realisation  and  minimising  Life Cycle Costs (LCC) (Zimmerman et al., 2019; Bahoria et al., 2025). The  threshold  of  50  million  USD  (for  highway  projects)  and 40  million  USD  (for  bridge  projects)  are  specific  to  the  USA Federal Government Procurement Law, under the National Highway  System  program;  they  are  not  universally  applicable,  as procurement and VE/VM mandates vary across jurisdictions. For clarity,  this  study  specified  that  these  requirements stem from the USA Department of Transportation (DOT) regulation and can vary in other countries where VM is implemented.

However,  despite  this  requirement,  VM  adoption  remains surprisingly  low,  accounting  for  less  than  7%  of  the  estimated $4.6  trillion  spent  on  highway  construction,  as  reported  by  the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) (Shen et al., 2015). A strong example of Value Management (VM) leading to cost savings is  the  tunnelling  project  in  KSA  (Baghdadi,  2024).  VM  was systematically  applied  to  assess  alternatives  in  design,  material selection and construction techniques. Through functional analysis,  brainstorming,  and  lifecycle  cost  assessment,  the  project team optimised structural sections, stormwater systems and mechanical-electrical designs. Key majors include adopting shotcrete  for  confined  spaces,  refining  reinforcement  details  and selecting eco-friendly materials. The outcome was a SAR 13.89  million  reduction  in  overall  costs  while  also  improving long-term sustainability. This project  demonstrates  how  VM, when  applied  rigorously,  can  deliver  both  environmental  and economic benefits. Comparable VM  applications have been reported  internally,  such  as  irrigation  in  Kuwait  (43.8%  savings) (Suleiman  and  Shahid,  2024)  and  housing  projects  in  Iran  (16% savings)  (Ghasemi-Honari  and  Sobhiyah,  2025),  reinforcing  the global value of VM in infrastructure delivery.

The VM process consists of six distinct phases. It begins with information  gathering,  where  relevant  project  data  is  collected (Gökalp  et  al.,  2021;  Kineber  et  al.,  2021).  This  is  followed  by function analysis,  which identifies  essential  project  functions.  The creativity  phase  generates  innovative  solutions,  which  are  then evaluated  for  cost-effectiveness  and  feasibility.  The  development phase refines and finalises solutions, culminating in the presentation phase, where results are communicated to stakeholders.

Through value and function analyses, VM determines the costeffectiveness and feasibility of proposed alternatives, seeking to meet or  exceed  project  requirements  at  the  lowest  possible  cost.  Value enhancement is achieved by boosting performance, reducing costs, or  combining  both  approaches  (Dell'Isola,  1997;  Dell'Isola,  1982; Zhang  et  al.,  2025).  Furthermore,  VM  integrates  sustainability considerations into design and construction, encompassing energy efficiency, waste minimisation, air and water quality, recyclable and renewable materials, durability, disposal considerations,  low  LCC,  and  user  comfort.  By  incorporating these  aspects,  VM  contributes  to  more  sustainable  and  resilient infrastructure  development.  Despite  the  demonstrated  benefits  of VM,  there  is a noticeable lack of  research  that  applies  VM systematically  to  tunnelling  projects  while  explicitly  integrating sustainability metrics and Life Cycle Cost (LCC) analysis (Huymajer  et  al.,  2022;  Tafida  et  al.,  2024).  This  gap  highlights the  need  for  a  decision-making  framework  tailored  to  complex underground  infrastructure  projects  that  balances  performance, cost, and environmental considerations.

This  paper  investigates  the  application  of  Value  Management (VM) to tunnelling projects, focusing on decision-making support systems. The proposed methodology integrates established sustainable criteria to inform business decisions. These sustainability  criteria,  outlined  in  Table  1,  provide  the  evaluative framework for comparing design alternatives and guiding the VM analysis.  A  comprehensive  VM  study  has  been  conducted  on  a significant tunnelling project, incorporating LCC analysis. Notably, the design of the study and analysis of alternatives exceeds typical VM requirements, featuring complete preliminary designs for each tunnelling option.

This  rigorous  approach  yields  valuable  insights  for  engineers, researchers, and top management. Additionally, the study highlights critical  technical  aspects  of  tunnelling  design  essential  for  VM analysis, enabling optimal design selection and enhanced sustainability. By adopting VM, the findings can inform decisionmaking for governmental and private entities involved in sustainable infrastructure  development.  The  findings  presented  in  this  study have global  implications,  offering  a  transformative  framework  for tunnelling projects (Aghimien et al., 2018). Unlike conventional VM applications that focus solely on cost optimisation, this study offers a comprehensive  methodology  that  balances  cost,  function,  and sustainability within a tunnelling context. It contributes a replicable  model  for  integrating  VM  and  sustainability  in  earlystage infrastructure design decisions. The research demonstrates the potential of VM to enhance sustainability and decision-making in infrastructure development.

The  tunnelling  projects  offer  unique  challenges  compared  to other infrastructure works. They are typically capital-intensive with costs running into billions, and are implemented under conditions of high uncertainty owing to complex geological formations, safety hazards and difficult site accessibility (Singh et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2024). These projects face higher risks of delays, safety incidents and budget overruns compounded  by  the need for sophisticated mechanical, structural and electrical systems operating in constrained  underground  environments.  Additionally,  tunnelling often has long operational lifespans, meaning that poor decisions in the design stage can  lock projects into unsuitable costs  and environmental  impacts  for  decades  (Benardos and  Skenderas, 2025; Petroutsatou et al., 2021).

Value management (VM) offers a systematic, function-oriented approach  that  directly addresses  these  challenges  by  enabling multidisciplinary teams to identify and assess alternatives early in the design phase (Thneibat and Al-Shattarat, 2023; Gabr et al., 2024). Through structured function analysis, life cycle cost (LCC) assessment, and sustainability assessment, VM  can improve tunnelling designs to reduce risks, cut costs, and increase environmental  performance  (Rwamuhinda,  2024).  Whereas  VM has  been  widely  applied  in  highways,  bridges  and  buildings,  its underutilisation  in  tunnelling  is  a  critical  gap  given  the  sector's complexity and strategic importance. Thus, applying  VM  to tunnelling can improve decision-making and uncertainty management, balance performance and cost, and integrate sustainability into underground infrastructure, setting a new standard  for  resilient  and  efficient  project  delivery.  To  address the identified gap, this study investigates three research questions.  First,  how  can  Value  Management  be  systematically applied to support sustainability-oriented decision-making in tunnelling  projects?  Second,  which  design  and  evaluation  criteria are most relevant for comparing sustainable tunnelling alternatives within a Value Management framework? Third, to what extent can the integration of weighted evaluation and Life Cycle Cost analysis improve value outcomes and cost efficiency in tunnelling projects? Accordingly, this study contributes to infrastructure sustainability decision-making  in  three  ways.  First,  it  develops  a  tunnellingfocused VM  application that goes beyond  conventional cost reduction by integrating sustainability criteria into alternative evaluation. Second, it combines weighted multi-criteria assessment with Life Cycle Cost analysis to support more rigorous early-stage design decisions. Third, it provides an applied decision-support framework that can inform similar underground infrastructure projects in contexts where sustainability and long-term value must be balanced.

## 2 Research background

## 2.1 VM and the sustainable built environment industries

The significance of sustainability concepts in promoting longterm environmental, economic, and social health is welldocumented  in  the  literature  (Oke  et  al.,  2015;  Kineber  et  al., 2022d;  Kineber  et  al.,  2023b).  Sustainable  practices  are  important for preserving resources, enhancing the quality of life, and ensuring an ecosystem is viable for future generations. However, translating sustainability goals and policies into actionable strategies for projects is highly complex and complicated (Aarseth et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2023).  To  achieve  sustainability,  stability  requirements  must  be established  within  social,  economic,  and  environmental  aspects (Martens and Carvalho, 2017; Oke et al., 2015; Oke et al., 2024a). In the construction industry, the growing focus on sustainability has sparked a quest for practical methods to integrate this concept into existing  operational  frameworks  (Abidin  and  Pasquire,  2007;  AlOtaibi and Kineber, 2023; Kineber et al., 2023a). Thus, companies wishing to achieve sustainable progress and fulfil innovative corporate  social  responsibility  have  deployed  Value  Management (VM) at critical strategic stages (Fewings and Henjewele, 2019).

Value  Management  (VM)  is  a  systematic  method  aimed  at enhancing  financial  value  by  providing  required  functions  at  the lowest cost, while adhering to quality and sustainability standards (Kineber et al., 2020b).  Current perspectives emphasise clarifying, identifying,  and  validating  customer  expectations  and  priorities early  in  the  project  process  (Kineber  et  al.,  2020a).  Traditionally, VM is  aligned  with  the  briefing  phase  of  the  project  (Ann  et  al., 2007).  However,  its  practical  application  in  construction  projects remains unclear (Kineber et al., 2023c).

The assessed project is a large-scale tunnelling initiative in KSA (Allouzi  and  Al  Jaafreh,  2023;  Klingmann,  2023;  Ibrahim,  2025), undertaken to improve cost-efficiency and sustainability in transport infrastructure. Given the vital role of tunnels in ensuring  efficient  and  safe  mobility,  the  project  applied  VM  as  a structured  methodology  to  optimise  design,  material  use  and construction  techniques.  The  VM  study  involved  experts  from consultancy, engineering and academia, who evaluated alternatives  via  lifecycle  cost  analysis  and  sustainability  criteria

VM practices typically involve planned workshops ranging from half a day to 5 days. The duration varies depending on factors such as the scope, size, and objectives of a project. The practical approach of the Society of Value Engineers to VM involves a three-stage method: pre-study,  value  study,  and  post-study.  The  value  study  phase comprises six stages: information, function analysis, creative, evaluation, development, and presentation (Kineber et al., 2024a). Each  stage  builds  on  the  previous  one,  systematically  improving project value. Notably, the development, information, and presentation  stages  can  be  conducted  outside  of  the  workshop setting,  potentially  virtually,  and  more  efficiently.  This  flexibility allows for adaptability in VM implementation (Kineber et al.,  2022e).

Although the concept of sustainability may not be consistently emphasised in VM studies, the dimensions of sustainability-such as waste minimisation, creating a vibrant indoor environment, energy efficiency, user comfort, aesthetic impacts, and water and air quality, along  with  minimising  LCC-are  frequently  considered  (Ismaeil, 2024;  Sharma  et  al.,  2023).  The  understanding  and  application  of sustainability principles in VM studies can be subjective, depending on  the  intended  goals  of  a  client,  project  requirements,  team awareness,  and  time  limitations  (Oke  et  al.,  2022b;  Quildon  and Pun,  2024).  For  instance,  sustainability  considerations  have  been highly prioritised in various UK  infrastructure  developments, notably  in  the  sustainable  construction  project  at  Crianlarich, Stratfillan,  where  VM  is  deployed  to  align  with  community  and societal objectives (Bates et al.,  2025; Mell, 2021). Other examples include the (Fair et al., 2025).

In  this  study,  the  'sustainable  built  environment  industries' specifically refer to construction-related sectors, e.g., building projects, transportation and highway systems, tunnelling, renewable  energy  infrastructure,  irrigation  and  water  works  and industrial  estate  development  (Oke  et  al.,  2022a;  Kineber  et  al., 2022b).  These  industries  are  central  to  advancing  sustainability, since they integrate social, environmental and economic considerations  into  infrastructure  delivery  (Kineber  et  al.,  2020b). VM plays a major role in these sectors by aligning project objectives with sustainable outcomes, optimising resources and lessening life cycle costs while maintaining good quality.

Thus,  by  promoting  the  adoption  of  the  VM  concept  among major clients, a sustainable process for decision-making in construction  could  be  established  (Ojo  et  al.,  2022;  Kamranfar et  al.,  2022).  Additionally,  Kineber  et  al.  (2022f) identify  several advantages of using VM to promote and implement sustainability concepts,  including  enhancing  operational  resources  and  custom tools, increasing applications, and improving maintenance. Integrating  sustainability  with  VM  is  significantly  enhanced  by four key factors: a formal and structured VM study, multidisciplinary stakeholder involvement, incorporating sustainability as a primary project goal, and comprehensive accounting  of  total  project  execution  costs  (Orieno  et  al.,  2024; Blak Bernat et al., 2023). Therefore, adopting sustainability through VM is both recommended and feasible (Abidin and Pasquire, 2005).

## 2.2 Study gap analysis

Through analysis and review at various stages, VM minimises total costs, enhances project quality and value, and shortens project duration,  ultimately  achieving  a  win-win  solution  between  the private  and  public  sector  (Kušnírová  et  al.,  2024;  Alsadie  and Alsulami,  2024;  Kineber  et  al.,  2021).  Kim  et  al.  (2016) argued that utilising VM's  systematic process can be beneficial for generating  cost-effective  alternative  designs.  However,  there  is  a scarcity of VM studies that focus on this area. Thus, this study seeks to address this deficiency by employing a systematic VM process to broaden the scope of roadway projects on tunnelling, demonstrating its benefits (Benardos and Skenderas, 2025; Subramanya et al., 2022). The VM design process and the alternatives utilised in this study are expected to provide decision-makers and engineers with guidelines for expanding roadway projects on soft terrain.

The application of VM  in sustainable building projects demonstrated  how  VM  practices  were  implemented  to  provide alternatives  for  planning,  specification,  design,  and  local  material usage,  reducing  construction  costs  without  compromising  quality (Nasereddin and Price, 2021; Waqar et al., 2023). In a typical VM study, a value engineer or contractor conducts the analysis. Following  a  thorough  assessment,  the  value  engineer  evaluates the  need  to  reduce  project  scope  or  cost  by  employing  specific construction  methods,  modifying  the  design  process,  or  utilising approved techniques that maintain quality.

The traditional management techniques related to occupational health and safety (Parsamehr et al., 2023; Beckel and Fisher, 2022). They highlighted the substantial inadequacies of existing methods in ensuring construction safety, emphasising  their limitations in effectively addressing  safety concerns  within  the industry.  By applying  VM  techniques  to  address  irrigation  water  shortages  at the Cana-Tail end (CT), El-Nashar and Elyamany (2018) demonstrated  the  potential  of  VM  as  a  robust  problem-solving approach.  The  results  highlighted  the  ability  of  VM  to  identify targeted solutions aligned with the primary objectives of the system.

Previously, Mahdi et al. (2015) and Ahire (2016) underscored the advantages of VM in construction projects. Their studies found that  VM  yielded  immediate  savings  in  construction,  primarily through reduced costs per square foot of contact area and demonstrated its effectiveness as a comprehensive problemsolving  approach,  enhancing  functional  value  to  achieve  project objectives.  However,  evaluation  of  the  use  and  role  of  VM  in building  projects  for  the  social  security  organization,  focusing  on the  design  and  execution  of  the  Iranian  housing  management company,  revealed that applying VM  in  the early stages of design-such  as  generating  design  options  during  the  conceptual phase  and  before  final  planning-plays  a  crucial  role  in  achieving significant  value  savings  (Soltani  et  al.,  2024;  Karamoozian  and Hong, 2023).

Emami et al. (2017) provided an overview of the key aspects of VM  application in the design and execution of networks, highlighting variations in achievements and outcomes. These insights  have  proven  to  be  highly  beneficial  for  the  design  and execution of building projects in various countries. Other researchers  have  made  significant  contributions  to  the  VM  field. Bhirud  (2016) and  Ameyaw  et  al.  (2023)identified  some  critical factors influencing  the  application  of  VM  with  emphasis  on construction  and  highlighted  key  problem  areas  within  building projects. Building on this foundation, Qiu (2019) proposed a novel theoretical model integrating VM  into space systems. This innovative approach optimised design, enhanced functional value, and improved cost management for space system projects.

Researchers  have  demonstrated  the  effectiveness  of  VM  in reducing  costs  and  enhancing  project  efficiency.  For  instance, Varghese  (2017) argued  that  industrial  implementation  of  VM could result in significant cost savings, potentially reducing expenses  by  up  to  15%.  To  support  this  claim,  he  employed  an interaction  matrix  to  assess  the  impact  of  VM.  Additionally,  the study highlighted that design strategies and construction methods aimed  at achieving specific LEED  credits  could  inadvertently increase  baseline  safety  risks  (Stephen  et  al.,  2025).  Hence,  the benefits  of  integrating  VM  in  the  creative  phase  lie  in  making innovative  solutions  feasible.  Al-Anzi  et  al.  (2017) achieved  a 43.84%  cost  reduction  in  irrigation  projects  in  Kuwait  City  by applying VM,  showcasing  its potential for innovative design development  while  reducing  expenses.  Another  notable  example is the Ramhormoz project, where Emami et al. (2017) employed VM techniques. The VM  team  proposed  modifications to lateral drainage systems, resulting in estimated cost savings of 3.2 billion USD  (16% of the total project cost) and accelerating the project timeline.

In the same vein, researchers have demonstrated the effectiveness  of  VM  and  VE  in  other  sectors  other  than  building infrastructure.  For  instance,  Hossam  El-Din  and  El-Maaty  (2018) showcased  the  accumulated  experience  of  applying  VM  in  the transportation sector and highlighted its successful implementation  in  roadway  projects,  both  in  construction  and scientific research. Similarly, studies by Rachid  et al. (2015), Desai  et  al.  (2016),  and  Tom  and  Gowrisankar  (2015) applied VM in diverse contexts,  yielding  significant  benefits.  Notably,  the application of VM resulted in substantial cost savings and improved efficiency, as highlighted in the previous studies above. Rachid et al. (2015) found that VM  favoured  RO  seawater indirect solar desalination  over  MED  for  Mediterranean  countries  and  GCC nations. In ship construction, Desai et al. (2016) reported significant  reductions  in  rework  and  project  schedule,  saving  Rs 78 lakhs. Furthermore, Tom and Gowrisankar (2015) demonstrated the  potential  of  VM  in  enhancing  quality  and  reducing  costs  in housing construction projects.

Ilayaraja and Eqyaabal (2015) and Malek et al. (2025) contended that  VM,  combined  with  enhanced  decision-making,  ensures  the optimal use of the client's funds while achieving the required levels of  function  and  quality.  The  success  of  VM  lies  in  its  ability  to identify  opportunities  to  eliminate  unnecessary  costs,  while  still maintaining or exceeding expectations in terms of reliability, quality, performance, and other critical factors (Venkataraman and Pinto, 2023). Research on VM has primarily focused on selecting models for building projects and product design. However, design models within  construction  projects  tend  to  be  smaller  in  scope.  Studies highlighted the VM approach promoted by SAVE International, the official  society  for  VM  professionals,  which  provided  two  case studies  demonstrating  successful  applications  of  VM  techniques, resulting in cost savings and improved quality (Shen et al., 2015). In particular, the comprehensive VM studies led to a reduction in total project costs and offered a semi-generic recommendation matrix for alternative  designs  across  various  disciplines,  showing  significant benefits for housing projects (Abdelghany et al., 2015).

The  application  of  the  Pareto  Principle,  or  the  20/80  rule, showed that approximately 20% of functions accounted for up to 80% of the costs of a project (Blackman and Chan, 2013; Le et al., 2022).  These  key  functions  are  ideal  for  VM  analysis,  which  they emphasised  is  most  effectively  performed  during  the  project's planning  phase,  before being  handed  over  to  the  contractor. However, studies showed that a lack of management support was not  the  main  reason  for  the  limited  use  of  VM  as  a  building management tool. Instead, senior management must first recognise  the  benefits  of  applying  VM  in  building  management before its implementation (Kineber et al., 2023c).

Value  management  (VM)  experts  are  primarily  focused  on coordinating and understanding the schedule between management  and  design,  and  identifying  tools  that  can  help manage  the  construction  process  effectively.  Arivazhagan  et  al. (2017) argued  that  the  use  of  VM  could  result  in  significant  cost savings, with savings rates ranging from 20% to 25% of the total cost of a project. Their study revealed that awareness of bridge selection among project  managers and designers  was  at  53%.  Additionally, only  33%  were  familiar  with  cost  estimation  methods,  which  was concerning. Furthermore, the majority of the respondents admitted that they did not follow a systematic VE work plan as prescribed. Similarly, Parakhiya and Patel (2017) and Gunarathne et al. (2022) highlighted  VM  concepts  focused  on  reducing  project  costs  by suggesting  the  use  of  innovative,  locally  available  materials  to enhance the quality and sustainability  of a  project.  Their  findings showed that project costs could be reduced by 8% when appropriate materials  were  substituted,  particularly  in  cases  where  the  initial project  costs  were  high.  This  represented  a  significant  cost  saving when  compared  to the total project cost. A case study by Shahhosseini  et  al.  (2018) found  that,  after  analysing  all  available options  for  pipeline  transmission,  construction  costs  could  be reduced  by  up  to  41%  if  an  alternative  route  crossing  the  Reno Mountain was chosen.

Kissi et al.  (2016) investigated the challenges of implementing VM in government projects across  third-world  nations.  A  survey conducted among consulting and construction companies in Ghana rated  the  understanding  of  participants  on  some  22  challenges identified from existing literature. Factor analysis revealed correlations between these variables, ultimately extracting five key components that outlined the primary challenges VM faces in thirdworld  countries,  providing  valuable  insights  into  the  obstacles hindering  effective  VM  implementation.  Yan  et  al.  (2016) and Hongjie  and  Suryani  (2025) applied  a  VM  approach  to  identify and  evaluate  factors  crucial  for  the  efficient  management  and operation  of  budgets  in  the  hotel  industry  in  China.  Using  a mixed-method approach that combined quantitative statistics with expert panel  assessments,  they  analysed  Chinese  budget brands  publicly  listed  in  the  USA.  The  results  indicated  that,  of the six identified cost categories, energy and maintenance, as well as standard  room  costs,  achieved  optimal  values.  However,  human resources  and  lease  rentals  were  overestimated,  while  license  fees and  refurbishment  costs  were  also  found  to  be  overvalued.  The findings suggested that hotel investors and employees in the budget sector need to strike a balance to equitably allocate resources and improve customer experience. Future studies are needed to explore new approaches within VM practices to achieve even better results.

## 3 Materials and methods

Value  Management  (VM)  has  been  widely  adopted  across construction  and  infrastructure  organisations  to  improve  value, performance, and reliability while meeting stakeholder requirements.  In  this  study,  a  case  study  approach  was  adopted to examine the application of VM in a major tunnelling project and to evaluate how VM can support more sustainable project delivery. The  main  objective  was  to  enhance  the  sustainable  execution  of tunnelling works through a structured VM process that integrates function analysis, weighted evaluation, and Life Cycle Cost (LCC) analysis. The study involved a multidisciplinary VM  team comprising  tunnelling  specialists,  infrastructure  consultants,  and academics,  who  collectively  contributed  their  design  experience, technical judgment, and sustainability-related perspectives to generate, refine, and evaluate project alternatives.

A total of 12 experts participated in the VM study, including five tunnelling  engineers,  four  infrastructure  consultants,  and  three academics  in  civil  engineering  and  project  management.  The experts were selected based on a minimum  of  10  years of relevant  professional  experience,  direct  involvement  in  large-scale transport  or  underground  infrastructure  projects,  and  recognised expertise  in  design,  construction,  maintenance,  or  VM-related decision-making.  Their  role  was  to  support  the  identification  of project  functions,  propose  feasible  alternatives,  and  develop  a weighted  evaluation  system  for  comparing  design  options.  To derive the evaluation weights, the panel used a structured pairwise  comparison  and  consensus-based  scoring  process.  The weights were discussed collectively, refined iteratively, and finalised  only  after  agreement  among  the  participants.  Although formal  inter-rater reliability statistics were  not  calculated, the structured workshop process and the multidisciplinary composition of  the  panel  helped  improve  consistency  and  reduce individual bias.

The  VM  study  was  conducted  in  a  virtual  workshop  format, which enabled direct interaction among  the 12 experts and facilitated  collaborative  discussion  throughout  the  analysis.  The workshop  followed  the  six  standard  VM  phases:  information, function analysis, creativity, evaluation, development, and presentation.  During  the  information  phase,  the  team  reviewed the project background, design constraints, stakeholder requirements,  and  sustainability  considerations.  In  the  function analysis  phase,  the  project  was  decomposed  into  its  principal  and supporting functions using the Functional Analysis System Technique  (FAST).  As  illustrated  in  Figure  1,  the  main  project objective  was  framed  as  enabling  tunnel  operation,  which  was further translated into key functional requirements such as supporting  loads,  ensuring  safety,  controlling  water,  providing ventilation, and allowing access. These functions were then linked  to  higher-level  performance  outcomes,  including  reduced maintenance, lower construction cost, enhanced sustainability, and improved  durability.  This  step  provided  a  systematic  basis  for connecting  technical  requirements  with  sustainability  and  cost considerations before alternative solutions were generated.

In  the  creativity  phase,  the  experts  proposed  alternative design solutions through structured brainstorming. These alternatives  were  subsequently  screened  and  compared  in  the evaluation  phase  using  a  weighted  evaluation  matrix  and  LCC analysis.  As  shown  in  Figure  1,  the  evaluation  matrix  included criteria such as risk minimisation, durability, ease of maintenance, schedule, and constructability, allowing the alternatives  to  be  assessed  transparently  and  systematically.  In parallel, the LCC calculation structure incorporated initial cost, maintenance and operation costs, and salvage or residual value, discounted over time to estimate the total life cycle cost of each design  option.  This  combination  of  weighted  multi-criteria assessment and present-worth LCC analysis enabled the research  team  to  compare  alternatives  not  only  in  terms  of initial cost, but also in terms of long-term value and sustainability performance. To strengthen the integration between VM and sustainability, the study adopted an integrated decision-support framework, also shown in Figure 1, in which the VM phases were explicitly connected to sustainability  criteria  and  LCC  components.  The  sustainability evaluation considered economic,  environmental, and  social dimensions,  together  with  issues  such  as  resource  efficiency, emissions  reduction,  maintainability,  resilience,  and  long-term operational performance. The outputs from the weighted evaluation  and  LCC  analysis  were  combined  to  support  the selection of the most appropriate alternative. The development phase  focused  on  refining  the  highest-performing  alternatives into  practical  design  proposals,  while  the  presentation  phase consolidated  the  results  and  recommendations  for  stakeholder review. Overall, the methodological structure presented in Figure  2 enhances  the  transparency  and  replicability  of  the study  by  showing  how  functional  analysis,  weighted  criteria, sustainability metrics, and LCC were integrated within a single VM  process.  In  addition,  the  framework  highlights  several measures  that  support  reproducibility,  including  the  use  of standardised  FAST  logic,  consensus-based  weighting,  presentworth LCC equations, systematic comparison of alternatives, and clear  documentation  of  the  decision  process.  This  approach provides a structured and reproducible basis for applying sustainable VM in tunnelling and other underground infrastructure projects.

## 3.1 Information phase

The information gathering phase involved systematically collecting critical project data, including reviewing project documents (drawings, bill of quantities, design bases, specifications, and consumer requirements) and estimating project  costs  (El-Nashar  and  Elyamany,  2018).  This  phase  aimed to  clarify  project  goals,  scope,  expectations,  and  requirements, allowing customers and end-users to articulate their needs explicitly  (Ab  Ghani  et  al.,  2021).  The  context  of  the  project, design role, budget, estimated cost, and limitations were meticulously examined. Following this, vital information pertinent to  each of  the  stakeholders'  fields  in  the  building  sector was presented (Malek et al., 2025).

## 3.2 Functional analysis

To improve methodological transparency, the function analysis phase  was  formalised  through  the  Functional  Analysis  System Technique (FAST), which decomposed the primary project objective  into  core  and  supporting  functions.  This  enabled  the research team to link technical requirements, sustainability considerations, and downstream cost implications before generating  and  evaluating  design  alternatives.  By  structuring  the functional relationships in this way, the FAST analysis provided a clearer  basis  for  assessing  how  each  alternative  contributed  to project value, long-term performance, and sustainability objectives.  Once  a  thorough  understanding  of  the  project  was achieved, functional analysis was conducted to identify the essential  functions  of  the  project.  This  involved  analysing  each component or part  to  determine  its  purpose  and  function  within the project. Additionally, it explored the relationships among these functions and how they contribute to fulfilling the primary function of the project (Aghimien et al., 2022). This phase logically outlined and achieved project standards, requirements, objectives,  and undertaking goals. It  identified,  developed,  and  classified  essential and secondary functions, enabling the definition and articulation of stakeholders' roles in relation to the primary objectives of the project (Jaapar et al., 2012; Galera Zarco and Moretti, 2025).

Consequently, primary and secondary functions were outlined  and  classified,  along  with  their  associated  costs.  A Primary  Function  (PF)  is  defined  as  the  essential  performance characteristics  that  the  selected  technical  solution  must  achieve (Mousa et al., 2021). The PF represents the core utilitarian feature of a product or design needed to meet the user's requirements. To determine a basic function, one must ask, 'Can the function be removed  and  still  satisfy  the  user?'  If  the  answer  is  no,  the function  is  considered  basic  (Al-Yousefi,  2008;  Waseem  and Shahzad,  2025).  Secondary  Functions  (SF)  are  the  performance characteristics that should be added to support the PF (Kelly et al., 2004; Thanarajasingam et al., 2024). SFs are necessary to enable the achievement of the PF, and without them, the PF cannot be fully  realised,  particularly  when  it  is  required  to  meet  codes, standards, or mandatory owner specifications. Lastly, the Higher Order Function (HOF) is the ultimate goal of the value study (Al-Yousefi, 2007; Lavi and Reich, 2024).

After  identifying  and  classifying  these  functions,  they  were interconnected to elucidate relationships, enhancing understanding of the required work field. The Functional Analysis System Technique (FAST) facilitated this process, employing 'How' and 'Why' logic to detect and arrange product functions or processes into hierarchical levels (Kelly J. et al., 2004; Leung et al., 2024). This approach proved invaluable in function analysis. The FAST methodology  involved  iterative questioning,  using  'How  do  I (verb)  (noun)?'  and  'Why  do  I  (verb)  (noun)?'  to  establish  a functional hierarchy. This effort focused on selecting functions for further analysis and measurement, typically achieved by associating function value with cost. The resulting value-to-cost ratio highlighted functional  efficiency,  defined  as  achieving  functions  at  the  lowest possible budget.

## 3.3 Creativity phase

Identifying areas of low value will facilitate the development of efforts aimed at enhancing their performance. This process involved exploring innovative ideas, both feasible and unfeasible, to amass a wide range of possibilities. The objective was to encourage creative thinking  through  the  focus  of  suggesting  numerous  alternatives rather than evaluating the ideas at this stage (Tutesigensi et al., 2021; Spellacy et al., 2021).

## 3.4 Evaluation phase

The  idea  assessment  stage  is  regarded  as  the  most  crucial phase in VM study. During this stage, all ideas and alternatives previously generated were subjected to thorough evaluation (Ab Ghani et al., 2021; Gunduz et al., 2022). This evaluation involved analysing each idea for feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and potential impact on the goals of a project. Criteria established at the outset of  the  VM  process  were  used  to  assess  and  prioritise  each suggestion  systematically.  This  rigorous  scrutiny  helped  ensure that only the most viable and beneficial ideas were considered for implementation, thereby optimising project outcomes and resource allocation.

## 3.5 Development phase

This phase was dedicated to developing a practical alternative that balanced performance and cost considerations, without being solely  dependent  on  them.  To  compare  alternatives  generated during  the  creativity  phase,  the  weighted-evaluation  method  was employed,  which  took  into  account  both  monetary  and  nonmonetary criteria (Lu et al., 2021).

Weighted  evaluation  is  a  structured  decision-making  process that  involves  evaluating  various  criteria.  This  method  included  a four-step procedure: Step 1 involved calculating the total life cycle cost  for  each  alternative;  Step  2  focused  on  determining  the weightings for each criterion; Step 3 involved listing and analysing  the  alternatives;  and  Step  4  involved  calculating  the value index for each alternative.

## 3.5.2 Step 2: selection and determination of the criteria weightings

In this step, the criterion used to evaluate the alternatives was selected. Then, the criteria were assigned relative weights or degrees of importance using the paired comparison technique.

A paired  comparison  and  an  evaluation  matrix  facilitated  a detailed  comparison  between  the  proposed  designs  and  the original design, allowing for the determination of the weight of each criterion by comparing one against another, as illustrated in Figure  3 (Raval  et  al.,  2025).  The  process  began  by  posing  the fundamental  question:  A  or  B?  The  answer  was  then  rated  as minor,  medium,  or  major,  assigned  one,  two,  or  three  points, respectively. The comparison continued by evaluating A Against C, then D, and so forth, until all criteria were compared and their importance assessed.

The  effective  alternatives  were  recorded  and  evaluated  against each set of criteria. Five ratings, ranging from excellent (5) to poor (1), were assigned to each alternative as detailed in Table 2. Following the collection of this data, a statistical analysis phase was conducted to determine the optimal rankings. These rankings were then multiplied by  the  weights  of  each  criterion,  and  the  resulting  scores  were summed to calculate the total function points for each alternative.

## 4 VM analysis

## 4.1 Information

During  the  initial  phases  of  the  tunnel  redevelopment  and extension, critical project data-including reports, design requirements,  and  technical  studies-were  thoroughly  reviewed to  establish  a  clear  understanding  of  the  project's  configuration and key components (Lee et al., 2023; Dick et al., 2023). This process aimed  to  clarify  the  project's  objectives  and  performance  needs, forming the foundation for the subsequent VM analysis. Relevant materials, such as project boards, specifications, and bill of quantities,  were  examined  in  detail  to  identify  high-cost  areas suitable for improvement. This analytical review highlighted components  most  vital to project success and informed the prioritisation  of  value-focused  alternatives.  With  a  well-defined understanding of the project's functional and financial parameters, the study advanced to the next phase, where alternative solutions were assessed for cost-efficiency and performance enhancement (Bennett and Mayouf, 2021).

## 4.2 Functional analysis

The VM function phase provided a clear, impartial grasp of the project  requirements.  This  logical  phase  defined  and  met  the standards and objectives of the project. Function analysis enabled the  articulation  of  the  multidisciplinary  functions  of  the  project. These  functions  were  described  using  a  concise  two-word  format that combined an 'active' verb with a 'measurable' noun, focusing on the objectives of the project (Jaapar et al., 2012; Khalife, 2023). A function  analysis  was  conducted  using  initial  cost  evaluations, historical  and  contextual  data,  and  a  cost  table  (Ben  Bouallegue et al., 2024; Su et al., 2021). From this, a function analysis table was constructed  to  define  each  function  using  an  active  verb  and  a measurable noun and to categorise functions as Basic, Secondary, and Required  Secondary  (Akın,  2021;  Ma  et  al.,  2026).  The  Basic function (B) identified the main practical feature necessary to fulfil the essential need of a user. The Required Secondary function (RS) was essential for supporting the basic function, and without it, the primary function would not be achieved. Both the RS and Basic (B) functions can be understood independently of the Secondary function (S).

When  the  function  analysis  table  is  finished,  the  functional objectives of the project are adopted to be concerned with business  development  aimed  at  achieving  the  aim  and  objectives of the project (Almeida et al., 2022; Shaukat et al., 2022). Accordingly,  the  most  used  project  objectives  by  the  client  were identified to be the highest goal to achieve the development of ideas, as  follows  in  the  circle  of  sustainability  in  the  following  form (see Figure 4).

## 4.3 Creativity phase

During  the creativity phase, efforts were directed  toward generating design alternatives that aligned with the essential functions  defined  through  functional  analysis.  These  ideas  were developed  and  conceptually  tested,  particularly  in  relation  to  a closed  section  configuration  (Tutesigensi  et  al.,  2021;  Spellacy et  al.,  2021).  The  VM  process  incorporated  a  range  of  innovative techniques-such as Synectics, structured brainstorming, and deep thinking-to stimulate the creation of both imaginative and practical solutions. A constructive environment was fostered throughout  the  process  to  encourage  innovation  and  maintain  a high level of engagement, while minimising unproductive critique or  negativity  (Sun  et  al.,  2024).  This  atmosphere  promoted  the development  of  inventive  solutions  aligned  with  the  project's functional  goals.  Emphasis  was  placed  on  distinguishing  between economic and investment sustainability and broader environmental and community-oriented sustainability. General brainstorming techniques were employed to promote free-flowing idea generation,  including  unconventional  concepts  that  might  not initially  appear  feasible,  thereby  expanding  the  range  of  potential alternatives, i.e., structural analysis and Mechanical, Electrical, and Plumbing (MEP) systems (Lavikka et al., 2021).

## 4.3.2 MEP

The  site  work  began  with  reviewing  options  for  HVAC  and mechanical  system  design.  The  siting  of  variable  refrigerant  flow outdoor units was weighed between ground-level and underground placement.  In  the  final  design,  these  were  placed  underground, mounted  on  the  electrical  substation,  releasing  any  heat  from  a location where limited ground space can be devoted to aesthetic and other purposes. The facility was ventilated to protect the longevity and  efficiency  of  the  HVAC  equipment,  which  was  crucial  for maintaining  the  ambient  environmental  standards  (Asim  et  al., 2022; Chikwendu  et al., 2025). The installation of electrical systems and mechanical components, such as generators, was also planned at ground level, because access and maintenance would be easier. However, a plan with an underground setting was preferred to maximise the use of land at the surface level. As already stated, this setting  required  a  robust  ventilating  system  operating  within  the working capacity and safety threshold. Hence, it also explained that MEP design involved complex trade-offs.

Concerning  the  mechanical  design  in this proposal, it is important  to  note  that  certain  methods  are  considered  using  jet fans  at  the  lower  levels  of  the  tunnel  to  handle  the  air  quality, particularly for the generator locations and AC outdoor units. The second design modified the tunnel profile and positioning equipment  to  allow  for  maximum  space  and,  hence,  operational efficiency,  in  line  with  the  MEP  strategy  (Yan  et  al.,  2024;  King, 2018). The drainage systems would address stormwater and spillage management  through  efficiency measures, with two different collector  pipe  alternatives  being  considered  based  on  cost  and hydraulic  capacity.  While  the  first  alternative  is  costlier,  600  mm diameter pipes were favourable due to better handling of the water flow and their efficiency in the long run. The second alternative was using pipes of a diameter of 500 mm, which offers cost savings, but it raises a number of concerns over the safety margin and operational difficulties  that  might  arise.  The  design  methodology  of  MEP followed a careful selection process: cost evaluation and hydraulic performance studies to properly balance cost efficiency with operational  sustainability  of  all  decisions  (Ismaeil,  2024;  De  Luca et al., 2015). Strategic emphasis on making full use of underground spaces for core infrastructure effectively freed up ground-level areas to  more  pleasing  uses  without  compromising  the  integrity  or efficiency of the mechanical systems. Such integrated infrastructure development brings out sustainability, costeffectiveness,  and  holistic  design  in  the  construction  of  modern engineering projects.

## 5 Final results and VM phases analysis

To ensure clarity and consistency with standard  VM practices,  this  section  is  structured  according  to  the  typical six  VM  phases.  Each  phase  highlights  the  application  of  VM

within the tunnel project, with a focus on MEP, stormwater, and structural systems.

## 5.1 Information phase

During the information phase, relevant data and documentation were  reviewed  to  establish  the  foundational  understanding  of  the tunnel redevelopment and extension project (Stefańska et al., 2024; Großauer  et  al.,  2022).  This  included  technical  reports,  design specifications,  and  quantities  related  to  MEP  systems,  stormwater infrastructure, and structural elements. The objective was to identify the project's scope,  performance requirements, and high-cost components as initial targets for value improvement.

<!-- image -->

Engineering drawing

## 5.2 Functional analysis phase

A  functional  breakdown  of  each  system  was  conducted  to identify the essential (primary) and supportive (secondary) functions, particularly for structural and storm systems (Shi et  al.,  2024;  Ziari  and  Taleizadeh,  2025).  Functions  were  analysed using  the  Functional  Analysis  System  Technique  (FAST),  and weighted  scores  were  assigned  to  each  function.  These  scores were used in calculating the value index for design alternatives.

## 5.3 Creativity phase

Multiple  design  alternatives  were  conceptually  developed  for storm  and  structural  works.  Brainstorming  and  deep-thinking methods were applied  virtually  to  generate  ideas  that  fulfilled  the identified  functions  while  enhancing  sustainability  and  reducing cost (Anderson et al., 2022; Vinchon et al., 2023). Examples included adjusting  pouring  lengths,  modifying  reinforcement  details,  and introducing shotcrete as a backfilling material.

## 5.4 Evaluation phase

Each  design  alternative  was  evaluated  using  a  value  index formula, defined as:

Value  Index  (VI)  =  Total  Function/Total  Cost  (Karthikeyan et al.,  2021).

The alternatives were ranked by value index to identify the most cost-effective options.

- For MEP works, the original design was retained as it resulted in savings of approximately SAR 5.5 million.
- For stormwater systems, the original design showed the highest value and was recommended.
- For structural works, each section was analysed:

- o Section 1: Original design (VI = 134.46)
- o Section 2: Alternative design (VI = 126.91)
- o Section 3: Alternative design (VI = 83.85)
- o Section 4: Alternative 2 (VI = 811.79)

Additionally, the best-performing stormwater alternative had a value index of 15.32.

Tables 3-6 illustrate the scoring and cost comparisons for each structural section, providing visual justification for the selected options.

## 5.5 Development phase

Based  on  the  evaluation,  selected  alternatives  were  refined  to align with the project's performance, cost, and sustainability objectives  (Åström  et  al.,  2022;  Retzer  et  al.,  2022).  For  structural works, designs were finalised using a maximum pouring length of 15 m and incorporating shotcrete between the excavation boundary and the tunnel wall.

## 5.6 Presentation phase

The  final  VM  outcomes  were  consolidated  to  support  the selection  of  alternatives  that  offered  the  highest  overall  value  to the project. As presented in Table 9, the optimised design generated a directly traceable net saving of SAR 6,869,411, primarily driven by savings in MEP works (SAR 5,500,000) and structural works (SAR 1,567,385).  Although  the  selected  materials/construction-method alternative increased cost by SAR 197,974, it was retained because  it  delivered  higher  value  based  on  the  adopted  value index  assessment.  This  finding  reinforces  that  VM  in  tunnelling projects should not be interpreted solely as a cost-cutting exercise, but rather as a structured decision-support approach that balances cost, performance, constructability, and long-term sustainability. In addition, the integration of the preferred alternatives with architectural and operational systems, including lighting and ventilation,  ensured  functional  compatibility  across  the  project. Sustainability considerations, such as material reuse, waste reduction,  and  long-term  cost  efficiency,  also  informed  the  final selection process (Casciani et al., 2022; Bosher  et al., 2021). Accordingly,  the  final  VM  recommendations  contributed  to  a more balanced and sustainable tunnel design aligned with project budget constraints, operational requirements, and long-term strategic objectives.

## 6 Discussion

Recent advancements in the tunnelling sector have led to more efficient and sustainable methods, expertise, and materials. There is a growing need for significant sustainable development within the tunnelling sector of the construction industry (Huymajer et al., 2022; Moreno-Monsalve  et  al.,  2022;  Oke  et  al.,  2024b).  Moreover,  the importance of assessing sustainability and performance in practice is increasingly recognised. Strategies to incorporate sustainable development concepts must be developed (SpychalskaWojtkiewicz,  2020;  Kineber  et  al.,  2022c).  To  this  end,  VM  can effectively facilitate the achievement of sustainability in tunnelling projects. The paper evaluates VM tasks, suggesting the adoption of VM phases and activities that can effectively contribute to creating a sustainable project (Maceika et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2025). These VM  phases provide sustainability guidelines that should be integrated during the initial and conceptual design stages (Fewings  and  Henjewele,  2019).  Studies  on  VM  suggest  that alternatives developed through this process are expected to promote a safe and healthy environment for residents (Yates and Yates, 2003). Similarly, VM offers multidisciplinary opportunities to address issues related to the environment, community, and society to effectively enhance sustainability concepts throughout the lifecycle of building projects (Yu et al., 2018).

The traditional measure of the success of a project is well-known and comprises cost, time, and quality. However, this measure has been  criticised  in  recent  decades  (Lu  et  al.,  2015).  Therefore,  by implementing VM, construction companies can effectively balance cost,  time,  and  quality.  Additionally,  VM  helps  to  reduce  costs without sacrificing benefits (Abdelrahman and Nassar, 2024). Kelly and Male (2002) argue that measured time within the sustainable value system is a key component of the success of a project, and VM can  achieve  optimal  project  timing.  For  instance,  in  the  roadway sector, Atabay and Galipogullari (2013) apply VM as a sustainable approach, which reportedly saved up to 1 year of project time. These savings represent approximately 6% of the total project budget and 17% of the overall project schedule.

In the maritime sector, Tang and Bittner (2014) suggest that VM serves  as  an  effective  tool  for  enhancing  quality.  Lin  et  al.  (2011)

argue  that  VM  is  often  used  as  a  practical  approach  to  address challenges such as limited financial resources and strict scheduling in the construction industry. This is because financial analysis serves as the primary method for evaluating sustainable improvements and value  creation  within  a  company  (Kaczmarek,  2019).  VM  offers sustainability to be integrated into a construction project. Within the  sustainability  context  of  a  building  company,  it  has  been noticed that they face numerous challenges as they develop and have  a  positive  environmental,  social,  and  economic  influence (Lee and Mwebaza, 2020; Yu et al., 2018). These companies must also explore new  and  swift means  of sustaining customer confidence (Spychalska-Wojtkiewicz, 2020). Future VM activities provide companies and operators with the opportunity to make informed decisions that drive their success.  These  decisions  have  the  potential  to  create  a  longterm impact on corporate practices (Obiora et al., 2020).

Additionally, VM  activities can determine operating cost, traditional capital, and  project  duration  to  reveal the client's value systems. These firms could only focus on aspects of sustainability if  the  value  formed  surpasses  the  losses  caused (Malvik  et al.,  2021).  Accomplishments  of  the  successes  and through  enterprises  and  their  capability  to  boost  the  company's value over a long time hinge on holding an operative business model (Jabłoński and Jabłoński, 2016), and the model can be obtained via VE implementation activities.

VM has  become  an  essential  component  of  modern  building projects that have pursued a method to boost value (Cariaga et al., 2007; Baarimah et al., 2021). Unfortunately, the outcomes of studies on VM, including executed VM methods and established designs, are  barely  circulated,  impeding  knowledge  transfer  within  the transportation projects. Despite  the established benefits of its adoption in construction and civil engineering, very little documentation exists on the implementation procedures for highway  schemes  (Nsimbe  and  Di,  2024;  Leung  et  al.,  2023). Furthermore,  studies  on  VM  that  focus  on  bridge  projects  are relatively  low.  Thus,  it  is  essential  to  closely  study  the  potential of implementing VM.

## 6.1 Information phase

In  the  practice  of  VM,  the  data  phase  plays  a  crucial  role  in supporting  and  facilitating  VM  implementation.  Each  VE  study typically  involves  three  categories  of  participants:  the  Head  of  the VM  facilitator,  VE  members,  and  decision-makers  (such  as  the proprietors  or  their  representatives)  (Rostiyanti  et  al.,  2023;  AlTekreeti et al., 2021). The role played by each member can positively provide improvements towards sustainability. In this phase, participants  also  define  the  procurement  method,  environmental considerations, project duration, and performance expectations.

Additionally, several green alternatives have been incorporated into the initial design stage during the information phase, and the customers are expected to update their goals. Thus, the objectives of a project significantly influence the outcomes and behaviour of VM participants. Similarly, the VM  leader plays a key role by implementing a strategic plan to enhance awareness and understanding  of  sustainability  (Le  and  Le,  2023;  Duran  and Mariñas, 2024). Effective communication between VM facilitators and clients strengthens the owner's motivation to embrace sustainability practices (Zainul-Abidin, 2008). A survey conducted by Mohamad Ramly and Shen (2012) indicates that at this  stage,  construction  workers,  along  with  facilitators,  provide essential  information  related  to  their  areas  of  expertise.  However, different participants may have identified certain limitations within the project.

## 6.2 Function phase

It  is  important  to  note  that  the  most  critical  aspect  of  the function  phase  involves  an  analytical  process  that  defines  and achieves the objectives, requirements, criteria, and desired outcomes  of  the  activity.  Additionally,  three  key  elements  of  this function phase-namely, space, task components, and outline-should  be  thoroughly  examined  (Kelly  et  al.,  2014;  Ao et  al.,  2022).  Furthermore,  this  phase  emphasises  how  the  owner, sponsor, client, and other key stakeholders in a project perceive and interpret  a  specific  activity,  ensuring  alignment  in  understanding and  expectations  (Oke  and  Aigbavboa,  2017).  Additionally,  this phase  seeks  to  create,  identify,  and  classify  both  the  primary  and secondary  functions  of  the  project,  ensuring  a  clear  distinction between essential  and supportive activities  (Mohamad Ramly and Shen,  2012).  As  a  result,  members  are  encouraged  to  outline  and document the functions of a tunnelling with a long-term perspective, planning for the next 50 years (Yu et al., 2018).

The primary and secondary functions, along with their associated  costs,  are  outlined  and  classified  to  provide  a  clear understanding of the sustainable components of a project. Additionally,  this  phase  incorporates  a  diagram  that  includes  the primary  construction  functions  and  objectives  identified  by  the owner.  In  this  context,  the  building's  sustainability  may  emerge as  the  key  function  and  primary  objective  (Yu  et  al.,  2018). Therefore,  sustainability  dimensions  can  be  integrated  into  the functions and objectives of a project, further enhancing its overall  goals  and  impact.  Additionally,  the  diagram  illustrates that sustainability dimensions-such as user comfort, accessibility, life cycle costs, environmental impact, and influence-are incorporated into the VM decision matrix, ensuring  these factors are  considered  in  the  decision-making process (Kelly and Male, 2003).

## 6.3 Creativity phase

At  this  stage,  it  becomes  evident  that  designs  (or  ideas)  are developed during the creativity phase to achieve the necessary and appropriate functions within an analytical tree structure. During this process,  VM  members  use  general  brainstorming  techniques  to explore, evaluate, generate, and test alternative  methods  and approaches for executing activities throughout the project (Tutesigensi  et  al., 2021;  Spellacy  et  al.,  2021).  At  this  stage, inventive methods and techniques such as Synectics, Brainstorming, and deep thinking are employed. The  VM facilitator  should  foster  a  supportive  environment  by  ensuring that repression and criticism among members are avoided. During the creativity phase, stakeholders have an excellent opportunity to achieve their objectives by encouraging innovative designs.  Innovation  is  one  of  the  key  drivers  supported  by  this practical approach to optimising resource use and recovery (Munyasya  and  Chileshe,  2018).  Additionally,  investment  and economic  sustainability should  be considered separately  from environmental and community  sustainability when  proposing alternatives,  solutions,  and  designs.  This  distinction  ensures  that all aspects are addressed effectively without compromising one for the other (Yu et al., 2018).

## 6.4 Evaluation phase

VM experts and interdisciplinary stakeholders should evaluate alternatives  to  achieve  the  project's  sustainability  objectives.  An appraisal,  judgment,  and  selection  process  must  be  carried  out for  the  suggestions  and  designs  identified  in  the  previous  creative stage. It is crucial to assess the designs in relation to their functional requirements. This argument concurred with the literature (Spellacy et  al.,  2021;  Sayadnavard  et  al.,  2022),  recognising  that  additional testing  of  recommendations  and  proposals  is  most  effective  in determining  how  to  achieve  the  project's desired goals. This process  helps  ensure  that  the  proposed  solutions  align  with  the project's objectives and sustainability requirements.

## 6.5 Development and presentation phase

The  VM  experts  are  responsible  for  generating  a  shortlist  of suggestions and proposals. These structured proposals encompass planning, fabricators, manufacturers, sketches, materials/ descriptions,  specifications,  drawings,  and  detailed  research.  The short-term design is regarded as a feasible, practical, and authentic solution (Kineber et al., 2024a; Ibrahim et al., 2023). Furthermore, this phase addresses the core weaknesses, strengths, and potential of the ideas and concepts through processes such as evaluations and life cycle  cost  analysis.  In  addition,  at  this  stage,  the  tactical  plan incorporates  every  component  and  function  of  the  development phase. The results are concurrent with Oke and Aigbavboa (2017). They  argue  that  this  is  the  most  crucial  phase  of  the  VM  study, emphasising the need for careful consideration and investigation of the presentation and development phases. In contrast, some experts view the presentation of suggestions as occurring during the final phase of VM. The goal of this stage is to present these recommendations  to  the  approving  body  for  minimal  feedback and final approval (Oke and Aigbavboa, 2017).

Although  this  study  has  demonstrated  how  VM  can  improve cost efficiency and integrate sustainability considerations in tunnelling  projects,  the  assessment  framework  remained  basically descriptive and  project-specific.  Upcoming  applications  would benefit from linking VM  results with formal sustainability evaluation techniques to ensure greater transparency, methodological  rigour  and  compatibility  (Netinant  et  al.,  2023; Ncube  and  Ngulube,  2024).  For  example,  embedding  the  life cycle  assessment  (LCA)  might  capture  environmental  impacts across construction, operation  and  end-of-life phases,  thereby extending the analysis beyond  cost and functional standards (Kumar  et  al.,  2025;  Bacheva  and  Raposo  Grau,  2025).  Likewise, the adoption of standardised sustainability indicators, such as those set  out in  ISO 21929 or ISO 14040 series, might offer a replicable structure for measuring environmental, economic and social performance  (Soratana,  2025;  Gachkar  et  al.,  2024).  Established scoring systems,  such  as  Envision,  CEEQUAL,  or  BREEAM infrastructure, can complement the process by translating sustainability  objectives  into  quantitative  metrics  that  are  also aligned with international sustainability criteria.

Recent studies, including Skoulidou and Kazantzi (2024), highlight  the  value  of  risk-informed,  indicator-based  techniques that explain uncertainty and resilience in infrastructure sustainability.  Integrating  such  techniques  alongside  VM  might help address long-term challenges in tunnelling projects, especially those related to durability, climate risks and operational  adaptability.  By  integrating  VM  within  a  structured sustainability framework, future studies can move beyond descriptive assessments concerning systematic, evidence-based decision-making  that  improves  both  technical  performance  and sustainable outcomes. The integration might strengthen the applicability of VM  as a decision-support tool and offer a replicable model for improving sustainability in complex underground infrastructure projects.

## 6.6 Theoretical and practical implementation

This study advocates for the implementation of a fundamental VM approach for tunnelling projects to support decision-making processes. The method used represents a new effort to develop a more rigorous technique  for  making  decisions  based  on  performance  criteria.  A detailed  VM  study,  including  Life  Cycle  Cost  (LCC)  analysis,  has been conducted for a proposed critical tunnelling project. The analysis and design of the alternatives in this paper go beyond the typical level of  detail  required  for  VM  purposes.  In  general,  a  comprehensive preliminary design of the superstructure has been carried out for all the  proposed  tunnel  alternatives,  which  include  various  types  of tunnelling  structures.  As  a  result,  the  findings  from  this  paper  are expected  to  be  valuable  for  researchers,  engineers,  and  decisionmakers.  The  study  offers  insights  into  key  methodological  aspects of tunnel design that contribute to VM analysis, essential for selecting the  best  design  alternatives.  Additionally,  it  highlights  important technical aspects of tunnelling design that play a crucial role in VM analysis,  aiding  in  the  selection  of  the  optimal  design  alternative. Consequently,  the  results  can  help  both  private  and  public  agencies involved in tunnelling design to make more informed decisions.

## 6.6.1 Theoretical implications

The current literature on construction engineering management has  placed  less  emphasis  on  sustainable  VM  implementation activities  and  methods.  This  research  contributes  to  the  existing body  of  knowledge  in  construction  engineering  management  by enhancing  the  understanding  of  sustainable  VM  methods  and activities  for  tunnelling  projects.  Few  studies  have  examined  the application  of  VM  activities  in  tunnelling  projects.  Consequently, the VM process is often underutilised, representing only 7% of total transportation spending in the USA.

Federal legislation mandates  the use of VM  for  projects exceeding $40 million, and many state Departments of Transportation  (DOTs)  have  followed  this  lead,  neglecting  VM implementation for smaller transportation projects. These smaller projects are often deprioritised for VM  analysis due to the misconception  that  the  effort  and  time  required  are  not  justified, especially  for  smaller-scale  endeavours.  However,  previous  VE studies on smaller transportation projects have  demonstrated their  ability  to  reduce  project  timelines,  deliver  cost  savings,  and enhance overall value [37].

VM Methodology and Tools-A sustainable VM approach has been  adopted  in  this  study,  where  project  functions  have  been identified  and  validated  using  the  FAST  technique.  The  design elements  contributing  to  sustainability  are  defined  early  on  to estimate  value  using  the  Weighted  Evaluation  Method  (WEM). While the selection of sustainable criteria and their relative importance in  WEM may involve some subjectivity, this approach  is  more  rigorous  than ad  hoc methods  and  rules  of thumb  commonly  used  for  value  estimation.  The  potential subjectivity  can  be  mitigated  by  ensuring  a  balance  in  the background  and  expertise  of  the  evaluation  stakeholders.  The technical  expertise  of  participants,  along  with  an  appropriate level  of  detail,  is  essential  for  conducting  a  meaningful  and sustainable VM analysis. The Life Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) in this study is based on both standard and innovative tunnelling designs, fully detailed, including typical maintenance and inspection activities.

Finally, this study makes a unique contribution by incorporating sustainable material selection criteria into the VM study, specifically during the evaluation phase analysis. This approach introduces new knowledge to both the VM and tunnelling sectors by providing fresh perspectives on sustainability. By integrating the evaluation of green materials into the VM  process, the study offers a deeper understanding  of  how  sustainable  material  choices  can  influence the overall project. The selection of environmentally-friendly, costefficient, and socially responsible materials not only leads to more sustainable  development  but  also  aligns  with  all  three  pillars  of sustainability:  environmental  protection,  economic  viability,  and social  wellbeing.  The  consideration  of  green  materials  helps  to minimise  the  environmental  footprint,  reduce  long-term  costs, and  improve  the  quality  of  life  for  communities  involved  in  or affected  by  tunnelling  projects.  This  integration  enhances  the traditional VM  approach  by  adding  a  layer  of  sustainability, making it more relevant for modern construction challenges.

Moreover, the study highlights that sustainable material selection  can  optimise  resource  use  and  reduce  waste,  leading  to more efficient and eco-conscious tunnelling projects. This advancement bridges a critical gap in VM  and tunnelling practices,  providing  a  framework  for  future  projects  to  prioritise sustainability from the material selection phase, which has traditionally  been  overlooked.  In  doing  so,  it  sets  a  new  standard for how  VM  can  be  applied to infrastructure projects with sustainability at the forefront.

## 6.6.2 Practical implications

This  study  offers  several  recommendations  for  tunnel  project owners  and  workers  seeking  the  success  of  sustainable  projects through  the  implementation  of  VM.  The  findings  contribute  to the  application  of  VM  in  Saudi  Arabian  tunnelling  projects  by highlighting the role of sustainable VM in minimising unnecessary  costs  and  improving  project  sustainability.  A  key takeaway  from  this  study  is  its  potential  to  assist  all  stakeholders in focusing on the core mission of the project-guided by the criteria used  in  the  Weighted  Evaluation  Method  (WEM)-through  the implementation  and  practice  of  VM  activities.  This  focus  can ultimately influence the sustainability level of the project.

The results of this study may serve as a guideline or benchmark to  address  common  issues  such  as  cost  overruns,  project  delays, sustainability  challenges,  and  unclear  specifications.  The  insights gained  will  provide  project  owners  and  employers  with  a  clearer understanding of how VM should be adopted and implemented to achieve sustainable outcomes.  Additionally, the findings offer essential information that will encourage construction organisation owners to engage in sustainable VM  practices, which are critical to the successful completion of their projects.

The proposed VM model in this study can be highly beneficial if applied correctly, as cost reduction is a key goal of VM analysis. By implementing  this  model,  organisations  can  reduce  costs  while simultaneously  enhancing  the  value  system  of  a  project.  As  a result, top management can strategically allocate their  VM resources  and  members  according  to  the  influence  of  sustainable VM phases. This approach not only increases the effectiveness of VM activities but also strengthens the commitment of management to achieving superior project sustainability.

## 7 Limitations and future research

Despite the achievement of the objectives of this research, the following are the limitations and future recommendations.

1.  Enhancing  the  adoption  of  VM  within  organisations  by establishing clear teamwork principles, which are essential for the successful implementation of VM  practices. Additionally,  integrating  Structural  Equation  Modelling (SEM)  could  provide  a  more  structured  and  quantitative approach to understanding the relationships between various  VM  variables  and  organisational  outcomes,  thus improving decision-making processes.
2.  Encouraging employees to submit VM proposals during the execution of large-scale projects to maximise the value of the process. Incorporating AI-driven tools could help in automatically  evaluating  and  optimising  these  proposals, making the process more efficient and data-driven.
3.  While this study utilised estimated costs (Est.), project life cycle, discount rate %, salvage value, annual maintenance, and operational costs in the 'Total life cycle cost calculation sheet,' future research should consider analysing additional factors such as lean maintenance, cost reduction strategies, quality improvement, market share growth, and key success factors. Integrating AI-based algorithms could further
4. enhance  the  accuracy  of  these  analyses  by  incorporating predictive modelling and real-time data processing.
4.  Although some studies suggest separating investment and economic sustainability from environmental and community  sustainability when  proposing alternatives, future  research  should  explore  the  application  of  rough set  theory  and  fuzzy  c-means  for  integrating  economic sustainability into the decision-making process. Moreover, AI can be utilised for real-time decision support, helping  to identify  the  most  sustainable  and cost-effective alternatives.
5.  Future  research  should  consider  incorporating  ecological concepts such as eco-technologies, resource efficiency, resource recovery, and the recycling of carbon and nutrients  into  VM  studies.  AI  tools  can  be  employed  to monitor environmental impacts and suggest improvements, while SEM can assess the impact of these factors on overall project sustainability.
6.  This  study  emphasises  leveraging  value  management  to achieve  project  objectives,  reduce  costs,  and  enhance  the project's financial and marketing performance. The integration of AI can enable predictive analytics to forecast  project  outcomes,  providing  better  insights  for decision-making.  The  approach  presented  in  this  study can  be  adapted  to  other  projects,  incorporating  different criteria  based  on  the  specific  requirements  of  the  project owner.  By  using  AI-based  solutions  for  real-time  data collection  and  decision-making,  VM  adoption  can  be more tailored to the specific needs of each project.
7.  As  this  research  focuses  on  applying  VM  to  sewer  projects, further studies should explore the sustainability aspects of sewer systems using the VM approach, such as defining sustainability criteria  and  calculating  a  sustainability  compliance  index  for decision support. Additionally, the adoption of AI-based monitoring systems can optimise the treatment systems, improving  both  their  efficiency  and  environmental  impact. SEM could also be used to model and validate the sustainability factors affecting the treatment process.
8.  These recommendations introduce the potential of integrating  AI  and  SEM  for  more  efficient,  data-driven decision-making in the adoption of VM.
9.  While expert-based scoring and LCC  inputs provide valuable insights, the study acknowledges potential variability in these assumptions. A formal sensitivity analysis was not conducted; however, this remains a recommended area for future research to test the robustness of the findings under alternative scenarios.
10.  Expert Judgment  Bias: The  analysis heavily relies on qualitative  input  from  a  panel  of  experts.  While  efforts were made to ensure diverse representation and consensus, subjective bias may have influenced the weighting, scoring, and final recommendations.
11.  Single-Case Context: As this is a single case study based on a specific tunnel  project, the  findings  may  not  be  fully generalizable to other types of infrastructure or geographic regions. Broader studies across different contexts  are  recommended  to  validate  the  applicability  of the proposed methodology.

12.  The cost figures used in the value index calculations were based on estimates rather than finalised contract values or actual  expenditures.  This  introduces  uncertainty  into  the analysis and may  affect the precision of cost-benefit interpretations.

## 8 Conclusion

The  comprehensive  VM  study  applied to this significant tunnelling  project  has  unequivocally  demonstrated  the  powerful impact of integrating VM methodologies to enhance both economic efficiency and environmental sustainability in large-scale infrastructure  developments.  Through  meticulous  analysis,  the study  assessed  various  design  alternatives  against  a  rigorous  set of  criteria-focusing  on  structural  integrity,  material  efficiency, sustainability,  and  cost-effectiveness.  This  approach  led  to  the identification of optimal solutions that not only met but exceeded  performance  standards,  achieving  a  total  cost  reduction of  approximately  SAR  6,869,411  million,  which  translated  to  a monetary saving.

These savings were realised through strategic alterations in design approaches, selection of materials, and adoption of advanced construction techniques. Moreover, the selected alternatives promoted  sustainability  by  incorporating  environmentally  friendly materials and  energy-efficient methods,  effectively reducing  the overall  carbon  footprint  of  the  project.  The  study  also  highlighted that the implemented design changes would yield lower operational and maintenance costs due to the durability and ease of maintenance of the chosen materials and methods.

This VM study has far-reaching implications, serving as a robust model for future infrastructure projects. It underscored the critical importance of embedding VM practices early in the project lifecycle to  maximise  potential  cost  savings  and  environmental  benefits. Furthermore, the outcomes  of this study provided actionable insights and  a blueprint for project managers  and  engineers, guiding  the  development  of  infrastructure  projects  that  are  not only economically viable but also environmentally responsible and resilient to future challenges.

Recommendations from this study include advocating for the broader application of VM across diverse infrastructural projects to leverage similar benefits in cost savings and sustainability. It is also suggested that research efforts should focus on continuous improvement in techniques and tools to enhance their effectiveness and adaptability. Lastly, maintaining robust stakeholder  engagement  throughout  the  VM  process  is  essential to  align  project  goals  with  stakeholder  expectations  and  ensure comprehensive value delivery.

In  essence,  the  success  of  the  VM  study  in  achieving significant  economic  and  sustainable  milestones  establishes  a compelling  case  for  the  routine  integration  of  VM  in  the planning  and  execution  of  infrastructure  projects,  ensuring they deliver optimal value while meeting contemporary needs and anticipating future demands. This study demonstrates the potential  of  integrating  Value  Management,  sustainabilityoriented  evaluation,  and  Life  Cycle  Cost  analysis  to  support early-stage decision-making in tunnelling projects. The findings from  the case study indicate that the  proposed framework  can  help  identify  alternatives  that  improve  value and  reduce  long-term  cost  while  strengthening  sustainability considerations. Future multi-case studies and sensitivity-based assessments  are  needed  to  further  validate  and  generalise  the proposed approach.
